Sales Intelligence & Coaching Bible
0) Executive Summary
What We’re Building: GoMotto (formerly “Otto”) is an AI-driven system designed to behave like an elite
sales professional and coach. It ingests call transcripts, messages, CRM data, and company SOPs, and
outputs real-time guidance (next-best actions, objection handling) as well as post-call coaching and quality
scores. Unlike generic script-based tools, GoMotto embodies the decision-making and adaptability of top
1% sales performers – adjusting behavior, strategy, tactics, and language dynamically based on context.
The goal is not to sound like a good salesperson, but to think and react like the best, ultimately
outperforming human reps in consistency and insight.
Defensible Philosophy: Great selling is a layered process of behavior → strategy → tactics → language
→ evaluation. Our philosophy prioritizes behavioral cues and judgment first, then strategic maneuvers, then
tactical execution, then precise wording – all continuously evaluated and learned from. This stands on
research-backed truths: top salespeople excel by asking more and better questions, listening more than
they speak, and adapting to buyer signals 1 2 . They control the “frame” of the conversation without
resorting to high-pressure tactics that damage trust 3 . Multiple strategies can succeed; the “wrong”
strategy is one that erodes authority, increases pressure on the buyer, breaks trust, or ignores context.
Our system will evaluate interactions via frame control, pressure, engagement, and context fit
signals, then learn and adapt, just like a great salesperson reflecting on their calls.
Minimum Primitives for an Elite System: To achieve this, GoMotto’s core must include:
• Context Awareness: Detect nuanced signals (buyer tone, hesitations, objections, power dynamics)
in real time.
• Behavioral Mastery: A repertoire of fundamental behaviors (pausing, probing, mirroring,
reframing, etc.) that can be combined appropriately.
• Strategic Maneuvers Library: A rich set of proven sales “plays” or maneuvers (e.g. pullback, risk
reframing, social proof) with logic for when to deploy each.
• Tactical Language Engine: Fine-grained control of wording and tone, with soft rules (not rigid
scripts) that adjust language to the buyer persona and context.
• Evaluation & Learning Loop: Robust scoring of each interaction on key dimensions (trust built,
pressure applied, frame maintained, etc.), enabling post-call coaching feedback and continuous
model improvement.
Every concept in this document is tied to implementable logic, data, or UX. We emphasize actionable
frameworks, schemas, and examples to ensure this “bible” directly informs GoMotto’s development. It is
comprehensive but concise – no fluff. The focus is on making the system truly elite but ethical: guiding
reps to better outcomes without ever deceiving or manipulating customers.

1) The Core Model: Behavioral → Strategic → Tactical → Linguistic →
Evaluation
Overview: GoMotto’s intelligence stack is organized into five hierarchical layers, each with distinct inputs,
decisions, and outputs. This layered model mirrors how an elite salesperson operates – from high-level
behavioral choices down to specific phrasing, with a feedback loop for learning. Below is a conceptual
diagram of the layers and their flow:
Behavioral Layer (actions & mindset: how to behave)
↓ uses context to choose
Strategic Layer (maneuvers & approaches: what play to run)
↓ implemented via
Tactical Layer (techniques & steps: how to execute the play)
↓ expressed through
Linguistic Layer (specific words, tone, style: how to phrase it)
↓ informs
Evaluation Layer (outcomes & signals: how it went and why)
↺ feedback loop back into all layers for learning and adaptation
Each layer is defined precisely as follows:
• Behavioral Layer: Decides how to act in the moment. Inputs: real-time conversational context (stage
of call, buyer’s emotional state, engagement level). Decisions: fundamental behaviors like talk vs.
listen, push vs. pull, ask vs. tell, pause vs. fill silence. Outputs: a behavioral stance or immediate
action (e.g. “pause and let buyer speak” or “ask a follow-up question now”). Failure modes: talking
over the buyer, not adjusting behavior when power shifts (e.g., continuing to push when buyer is
resistant).
• Strategic Layer: Chooses which sales maneuver or strategy to apply. Inputs: signals from Behavioral
layer + identified buyer situation (e.g. objection type, trust level). Decisions: selects one or more
maneuvers (e.g. a pullback disqualification, a value reinforcement, a reframing of the conversation)
best suited to the context. Outputs: a game plan (which maneuver to execute next and with what
objective). Failure modes: picking a strategy mismatched to the context (e.g. challenging the buyer at
a moment that needed empathy), or defaulting to a single approach for all situations.
• Tactical Layer: Determines how to execute the chosen maneuver step by step. Inputs: the selected
maneuver definition (from a playbook of steps) and real-time feedback (did the buyer answer that
question? Are they reacting positively?). Decisions: the sequence and method of execution – e.g., for
a pullback maneuver, the Tactical layer ensures the sequence “validate → authority statement →
soft disqualify → silence” is followed correctly. Outputs: a structured set of actions or conversational
turns (which questions to ask, points to cover, where to pause). Failure modes: getting the sequence
wrong (e.g. disqualifying without first establishing authority), or being too robotic in execution
(following steps even when buyer’s reaction suggests to pivot).
2

• Linguistic Layer: Handles the exact wording, tone, and style. Inputs: the tactical plan for what needs
to be said or asked, plus buyer persona info (e.g. homeowner vs. procurement officer) and company
SOP guidelines. Decisions: chooses specific phrases, words, and tone (e.g. whether to say
“affordable” vs “cheap”, whether to use casual tone or formal) to maximize impact without sounding
“salesy”. It applies soft rules – context-sensitive language guidelines rather than fixed scripts.
Outputs: one or several phrasing suggestions for the rep, calibrated to sound natural and
authoritative for the situation. Failure modes: using language that undermines the strategy (e.g.
apologetic or weak words that reduce authority), or misfiring on tone (too formal for a casual
customer, or vice versa).
• Evaluation Layer: Assesses the outcomes of interactions to inform learning and coaching. Inputs: the
call transcript/recording annotated with events (what maneuver was tried, buyer responses,
emotional tone shifts) and results (e.g. meeting set, sale lost, etc.). Decisions: computes scores and
diagnostics – e.g., Frame Control Score (did the rep maintain appropriate control of the
conversation?), Pressure Management (was the buyer appropriately pressured or too pressured?),
Engagement Level (did buyer engage more or less as call progressed?), Context Fit (were tactics
appropriate for the buyer’s stated needs and signals?), Trust Delta (did trust increase or erode
during the call?). Outputs: quantitative scores and qualitative feedback (which moments were
missteps, what could be improved) and updates to the model’s internal policies. Failure modes:
outcome bias (judging a good strategy as “wrong” just because the sale was lost due to external
factors), or mis-attribution (e.g. blaming the rep for a price objection that was truly out of their
control). We guard against this by focusing on how the decision was made and its effect on the buyer’s
receptiveness, not just whether the deal closed 4 5 .
Together, these layers ensure that GoMotto has a complete sales brain: it perceives and interprets context
(Behavioral), selects the right play (Strategic), executes expertly (Tactical), speaks with optimal wording
(Linguistic), and learns from every call (Evaluation).
What Actually Makes an Elite Salesperson (Foundational Truths)
Before diving deeper, it’s crucial to ground the system in research on what separates the top 1% sales
performers from the rest. This guides our design of each layer:
• Cognitive and Emotional Traits: Top salespeople exhibit high levels of curiosity,
conscientiousness, and humility, rather than the stereotypical aggressive extroversion 6 7 . In
a study of 1,000 B2B reps, 82% of top sellers scored extremely high on curiosity (a desire to deeply
understand the customer’s situation) 2 . They ask more and better questions and actively listen,
rather than just pitching. They are highly conscientious – they prepare diligently, plan their calls,
and follow through on promises 8 . Interestingly, they were 30% less gregarious than average
sellers 7 , meaning being overly friendly and relationship-focused is not their main approach;
they’re willing to challenge customers when needed. They maintain modesty (91% had medium to
high humility), giving credit to customers and team, which can foster trust 9 .
• Behavioral Patterns: Research by Gong.io analyzing thousands of calls found that top performers
talk less and listen more than average reps. The best sellers have a roughly 46:54 talk-to-listen
ratio, whereas average reps talk far more (often >60% of the time) 1 . Top reps also switch up
their approach more frequently during a call – one study found top performers make 54% more
3

conversational switches (changes in who’s speaking) than others 10 , indicating an interactive two-
way dialogue rather than monologues. They control the flow of conversation with tactics like
strategic pausing and asking direct questions, but they do so while making the buyer feel heard.
Importantly, they excel at active listening – summarizing and confirming understanding, which
builds trust.
• Frame Control and Authority: Elite sellers establish themselves as trusted advisors or “peer” to
the buyer rather than a subordinate vendor. They manage the “frame” of the conversation – the
perspective that defines the interaction. Research from the Challenger Sale showed that the highest
performers (especially in complex sales) are those who teach and challenge customers, leading
with insight and not being afraid to take control when necessary 11 12 . In fact, only 7% of high
performers were the classic “Relationship Builders” who just tried to please the customer, while
nearly 40% were “Challengers” who pressed constructively and controlled the sale 3 . This doesn’t
mean being pushy; it means not shying away from tough topics (like budget, timeline, or decision
process) and holding one’s ground in the face of tests. Top reps recognize subtle “status tests” from
buyers – e.g. a buyer comes late to a meeting or makes an offhand skeptical remark – as challenges
to their authority, and they respond calmly and confidently, signaling that they are a trusted equal,
not a desperate seller. They know when to push back vs. when to accommodate, which maintains
mutual respect.
• Adaptive Problem Solving: Elite sellers are strategy-flexible. They don’t rigidly follow a script;
rather, they have a mental toolkit of approaches and adjust on the fly. They diagnose the type of
situation: Is the buyer truly interested or just polite? Is the objection about price genuine or a
smokescreen for a deeper concern? Who are the decision makers? Top reps excel at this situational
awareness. They use frameworks (SPIN, Challenger, etc.) internally as guidelines but not as
straightjackets. They often run multiple “threads” in parallel – for example, continuing to build
value and insight while also testing for budget constraints. If one approach isn’t resonating, they
seamlessly pivot to another. This adaptability is a learned skill known as adaptive selling, which
research links to higher performance 13 . Our AI must mimic this by evaluating context continuously
and not getting locked into one script.
• Handling Uncertainty and Failure: Another trait is how top performers handle uncertainty. They
remain calm under pressure, have high emotional intelligence to regulate their own reactions, and
do not rush to fill every silence. They trust the process and know when not to push. Elite sellers also
engage in honest post-call self-evaluation. They are often “harder on themselves than any manager”
– extremely driven to refine their craft 14 . They attribute outcomes correctly: if a deal is lost, they
analyze whether it was something in their control (strategy, relationship, etc.) or an external factor,
and they adjust accordingly. This lack of complacency and continuous learning mindset is something
we’ll instill in the system via the Evaluation layer feedback loops.
In summary, an elite salesperson is defined by behavioral discipline, situational strategy, tactical
execution, linguistic finesse, and constant learning. This core model captures those aspects. Next, we
detail each layer starting with the most fundamental: the Behavioral layer, which is about what elite
salespeople do (their actions and reactions), not just what they say.

Behavioral Layer: What Elite Salespeople Do, Not Say
The Behavioral layer represents the unspoken actions and reactions that an elite salesperson employs
during a conversation. These are behavioral primitives – foundational moves like pausing, probing,
mirroring, changing pace – that form the backbone of effective selling regardless of specific methodology.
This layer is framework-agnostic and universal; it’s about human interaction fundamentals.
Key Behavioral Primitives and Patterns:
• Pause and Silence: Top reps are comfortable with silence. Instead of rushing to fill every gap, they
pause strategically, especially after asking a difficult question or after the buyer expresses a
concern. This creates a slight tension that often prompts the buyer to elaborate or reveal true
feelings. It also conveys confidence – you’re not desperate to jump in. (Behavior: Stay silent after a
pullback statement to let it sink in, rather than nervously rambling.)
• Probing vs. Restraining: Elite sellers know when to ask questions and when to stop asking. Early
in discovery, they probe deeply (open-ended questions, follow-ups) to truly understand the buyer.
But they also recognize signals when the buyer has had enough of questions (e.g. giving short,
irritated answers), at which point continuing to interrogate can backfire. At that moment, a top rep
transitions to sharing insight or summarizing rather than drilling further. (Behavior: If buyer’s
engagement drops with too many questions, shift gears to giving value or perspective.)
• Mirroring & Body Matching: Without even thinking about it, great salespeople often mirror the
buyer’s tone and certain words to build rapport (this can be verbal or physical in person). By subtly
mirroring key phrases (“I’m concerned your solution is too complex” → Rep: “Complex?” with a
concerned tone), they show they’re listening and prompt the buyer to continue 15 16 . They also
adjust their energy level to the buyer – e.g. if the buyer is analytical and soft-spoken, the rep
becomes a bit more formal and measured.
• Reframing and Redirecting: When the conversation goes off-track or gets stuck on a minor point,
elite reps reframe – they gently redirect to the bigger picture or a different angle. For example, if a
buyer is laser-focused on price too early, a skilled rep might say, “I hear price is top of mind. I’d like
to ensure you’re only investing in something that truly solves your problem. Let’s look at what an
ideal solution would need to do first.” This reframes to the problem-solving process, rather than
arguing about price right away. It’s a behavioral choice to not engage on a losing battle, but to set a
new frame.
• Disengage (Strategic Withdrawal): Top sellers sometimes deliberately step back – for instance,
they might say, “It might be that we’re not the right fit for you.” This pullback behavior (discussed
more in maneuvers) is counterintuitive to average reps. It’s done to reduce pressure and reset the
dynamic. Behaviorally, it means being willing to disqualify or walk away, which paradoxically can
increase the buyer’s interest if done with confidence. Elite reps also know when not to answer a
question immediately – if a buyer is throwing a curveball or trying to control the conversation, the
rep may delay answering and instead ask a clarifying question or turn it around. (Behavior: Don’t
chase every question; sometimes hold your ground by not directly answering on the spot.)
5

• Emotional Regulation: Another behavioral aspect – top salespeople keep their cool. If the buyer
becomes agitated or skeptical (“I’m not sure I trust your company’s experience”), the elite rep doesn’t
get defensive or visibly rattled. They might take a breath (subconscious behavior), maintain a calm
tone, and address the concern methodically. Their heart rate might spike, but behaviorally they
project calm authority. This helps de-escalate conflict.
• Active Listening Signals: Great reps give subtle signals that they are listening: saying “mm-hmm” at
the right moments, or verbally acknowledging (“I understand that concern”). They may label
emotions (“It sounds like you’re frustrated with your current system” – this is known as labeling in
negotiation, acknowledging the feeling 17 ). By doing so, they validate the buyer’s perspective.
These behaviors build trust because the buyer feels heard.
• Adjusting Pacing and Pressure: If the buyer is enthusiastic and talkative, a top rep will let them
speak at length (not cutting them off), injecting questions occasionally to guide. If the buyer is quiet
or hesitant, the rep might slow down, ask gentle questions, and not overwhelm them with chatter.
They modulate the pressure: for example, if they sense the buyer pulling away (engagement
dropping), they might actually ease off (maybe even hint at “it’s okay if this isn’t a fit”) to remove
pressure. Conversely, if a buyer is engaged but indecisive, they might increase positive pressure
slightly by creating urgency or asking for a small commitment. These adjustments happen in real-
time based on behavioral cues like tone of voice, speaking speed, and things like the buyer’s
breathing or sighs (on calls, sighs or long pauses can indicate hesitation or doubt).
Signals Elite Reps React To: Importantly, this Behavioral layer is driven by subconscious or conscious
recognition of micro-signals from the buyer:
• Power Shifts: If the buyer suddenly takes a dominant tone (“Let’s cut to the chase – what’s your
bottom line price?” in a commanding voice), a top rep notes this power play. Behaviorally, they might
pause (to not be steamrolled) and calmly reassert control (“Happy to discuss pricing – just to make
sure I quote you right, can we review your priority requirements?”). The pause and redirect is a
reaction to a power shift signal.
• Emotional Resistance: Signs like a change in the buyer’s tone (warm to cold), terse answers, or explicit
phrases (“I’m not convinced,” “This sounds too good to be true”) indicate resistance. An average rep
might plow ahead with their script; an elite rep will recognize “the buyer’s guard is up now.”
Behaviorally they may switch to a soothing approach: slow down, acknowledge the skepticism (“I
hear you – I’d be skeptical too, given your past experiences. Let’s unpack that.”), and avoid arguing.
They might also physically lean back (if in person) or modulate their tone to be more empathetic.
• Trust Signals: On the positive side, if a buyer starts volunteering information unprompted (“Actually,
our main headache is not price, it’s that our current provider is unreliable…”), this is a trust signal –
the buyer is opening up. A great rep immediately rewards that by listening intently and encouraging
them (“I appreciate you sharing that”). They might also subtly increase their authority posture here
by offering a quick insight (“You’re not alone; about 60% of companies we talk to mention reliability
issues 18 . There’s a pattern we’ve seen…”). This combination of listening and then delivering a
tailored insight cements the advisor role.
6

• Engagement Decay: If the conversation is losing energy (buyer’s answers become “yeah…sure…
maybe” or they start repeating objections), an elite salesperson senses this drop in engagement.
Behaviorally, they may take a bold step to re-engage: change the topic, ask a provocative question
(“Can I ask, is that the only thing holding this back?”), or even suggest a pause (“Would it be useful to
continue this later when you have more time to dig in?”). They resist the temptation to just push
harder with the same pitch when engagement is clearly waning.
• “Status Tests”: Some buyers will test a seller’s confidence, consciously or not. For example, the buyer
might make a slightly rude remark or challenge a claim (“I doubt your solution can really do that”), or
they might ask a knowledge-testing question. Top reps recognize a potential status test and respond
in a way that maintains credibility. They neither grovel (“Oh, maybe you’re right, I’m not sure”) nor do
they become combative (“Of course it can, you clearly don’t understand…”). Behaviorally, they stay
composed, provide facts or turn the question back: “It’s good to be skeptical. Others felt the same
until they saw it. Would it help if I shared how we do it?” This assures the buyer that the rep is not
intimidated and remains a problem-solver – passing the test by staying centered and assertive but not
defensive.
In implementation, the Behavioral layer of GoMotto will require the system to detect these kinds of signals
(via acoustic analysis, transcript cues, etc.) and have a set of behavioral policies to adjust. For example, a
policy might be: “If buyer has spoken < 30% in the last 5 minutes, stop and ask an open question” (preventing
the rep monologue). Or “If buyer gives two short negative responses in a row, consider a pullback maneuver”.
These are directly derived from how human elite sellers modulate their behavior.
This layer underpins everything: no matter how clever a script or strategy, if the rep’s behavior (timing,
listening, reacting) is off, the sale will derail. Thus, GoMotto first ensures the AI (and by extension the
human rep using its guidance) is behaving in a manner consistent with trust-building, authority, and
engagement control. Only then do we layer on specific strategies and tactics, which we cover next.
2) Buyer Psychology & Conversation Dynamics (Research-Backed)
Understanding buyer psychology is crucial for GoMotto to make context-aware decisions. This section
distills key principles from psychology and communication research that influence sales conversations, with
guidance on “What the model should do” for each.
Trust Formation and Authority Perception
Trust is the Currency of Sales: In any sales conversation, the buyer is subconsciously asking, “Can I trust
this person?” Trust has two main components: credibility (does the rep know their stuff and have a valid
solution?) and authenticity (is the rep honest and acting in my interest?). Research from Gartner found that
53% of customer loyalty is driven by the sales experience – more than price or product – indicating how
much a buyer’s trust in the salesperson impacts their decision 19 . To build trust, reps must demonstrate
reliability (do what they say), competence (understand the buyer’s problem and product), and empathy (listen
and acknowledge the buyer’s feelings).
• What the model should do: Guide behaviors that build credibility early (e.g. citing a relevant success
story or insight to establish expertise) and reliability (e.g. summarizing what the buyer said to show
understanding). It should also warn against trust killers like making unsupported claims or ignoring
7

the buyer’s concerns. If the buyer expresses skepticism (“I’m not sure I believe these results”), the
model should trigger a trust-building maneuver – perhaps offering proof (data, testimonial) or
acknowledging and addressing the doubt transparently. The model should also monitor the trust
delta: if it detects trust eroding (e.g. buyer’s tone turning cold, or repeating concerns), it should
adjust strategy (maybe slow down and address fundamentals).
Authority and Frame: Buyers assess the rep’s status and confidence as well. If a salesperson grovels or
seems overly eager to please, it lowers perceived authority. Conversely, a rep who respectfully sets an
agenda and doesn’t jump at every demand is seen as more of a peer advisor. Books like Pitch Anything
(Oren Klaff) popularized the idea of frame control – the person with the stronger frame (conviction in their
perspective) will lead the interaction 20 . Ethically, we want the rep to lead in a way that guides the buyer to
a good decision, not through manipulation but through confidence and insight.
• What the model should do: Ensure the rep establishes a frame early – e.g., by setting a collaborative
agenda (“Let’s start by understanding X, then we can see if Y is a fit”), rather than immediately
submitting to, say, “Just tell me the price.” The model should identify attempts by the buyer to
dominate or derail (like aggressive questioning or dismissive comments) and coach the rep to
maintain composure and reframe. For instance, if buyer says “You have 5 minutes, go,” the AI
might advise a bold but polite response: “I can certainly be brief – and if after 5 minutes you feel it’s
not a fit, we’ll end there.” This response meets the challenge without cowering, thus preserving
the rep’s status.
The model should track whether the rep is losing the frame (e.g., just reacting to buyer’s every whim) and
suggest corrections, such as asking a strategic question to regain control or even using a pullback if the
buyer is treating the rep poorly (signaling the rep is willing to walk if not respected).
Cognitive Biases and Decision Heuristics in Buying
Buyers, being human, rely on mental shortcuts and biases when making decisions. Our system should
leverage positive aspects of these biases to help the buyer make a beneficial decision, and mitigate biases
that stall decisions.
Some key biases and their impact:
• Loss Aversion: People tend to strongly prefer avoiding losses over acquiring gains 21 . The pain of
potentially losing something (money, security, time) looms larger than the pleasure of an equal gain
22 . In sales, this means buyers often fear the risks of a new solution more than they get excited
about the benefits 23 24 . They worry: “What if this change messes things up? What if I lose money
or reputation?” This can make them stick with the status quo even if your solution is better.
• Model action: Use loss aversion ethically to motivate action. The model should coach the rep to
reframe benefits in terms of avoiding loss. For example, instead of “You’ll gain 10% efficiency,” say
“Right now you’re losing time/manpower – staying where you are could be costing you $X” 25 26 .
Highlight the cost of doing nothing to make inaction scarier than action 27 28 . The model should
identify when a buyer is in “risk avoidance mode” (e.g. they voice concerns like those listed in
research: implementation concerns, budget anxieties, career risk 29 ) and supply the rep with
reassurance and evidence focusing on risk mitigation (like guarantees, pilot programs, ROI
evidence). It might also suggest a story or example of a company that “played it safe” and ended up
8

worse off, to trigger loss aversion in favor of change 30 . All of this taps into the bias to overcome
the inertia.
• Status Quo Bias: Similar to loss aversion but broader – people prefer things to stay the same, and in
B2B sales “no decision” is often the biggest competitor 31 32 . Research shows at least 40% of
deals in pipelines are lost to nothing changing 32 . Buyers rationalize sticking with the known (even
if suboptimal) because change itself has perceived costs and uncertainties 33 34 . Four causes are
identified: preference for stability, regret avoidance, action difficulty, and selection overload 35 .
Essentially, it feels safer to do nothing.
• Model action: Help the rep dislodge the status quo gently. The model should surface maneuvers
like “Unconsidered Needs” (raise a problem or opportunity the buyer hasn’t thought of) 36 and
contrast frames (compare the future if they change vs if they don’t). For instance, coach the rep to
say: “I know staying with what you have feels safe. However, companies that didn’t upgrade
experienced X negative outcomes, whereas those who did saw Y improvement” 37 . Provide
data to make not changing feel risky 27 38 . The model should pick up if the buyer says things like
“we’ve always done it this way” or “I’m not sure about making a switch,” which are status quo signals,
and respond with a strategic maneuver to challenge that gently (perhaps a Challenger Sale style
insight that stirs dissatisfaction with the status quo in a helpful way).
• Anchoring Effect: The first piece of information (especially a number like price) sets an anchor that
biases subsequent judgment 39 . For example, if the buyer has an initial price expectation or hears a
competitor’s low price first, everything you present will be compared to that anchor. Or if the rep first
pitches the highest package, that can anchor the buyer’s perception of cost/value.
• Model action: Manage anchors. The model might advise anchoring high on value before price –
e.g., emphasize the high cost of the problem or the high value of solving it before discussing what it
costs. If a buyer anchors on a low figure (“We were thinking of spending $5k”), the model should
trigger tactics like reframing the price (break it into monthly cost, compare to cost of problem) or
adjusting scope rather than just conceding. It could also coach the rep to set an anchor: for
instance, mention a higher reference point early (“some solutions cost six figures, but let’s see what
you actually need”). Essentially, ensure the first salient number or idea works in our favor. The model
should be aware of external anchors too – if the buyer references a competitor or a budget, that’s an
anchor to deal with. (We’ll cover price anchoring maneuvers in the playbook section.)
• Social Proof and Consensus: Buyers are influenced by what others (especially peers) are doing – a
principle of influence noted by Cialdini. If they know that “many homeowners on your street have
chosen this solar installer” or “X big company also adopted this software,” it reduces perceived risk.
Social proof taps into the fear of missing out and herd mentality.
• Model action: Provide social proof elements when trust is low or skepticism is high. The model can
suggest telling a relevant customer story (“One of our clients, a business similar to yours, faced the
same hesitation. They went ahead and saw Y results” plus a citation or offer to connect them as
reference). Or mention numbers (“Over 1500 customers have upgraded to this service in the last
year” – if true, of course). It should not overuse this (or it becomes generic), but in the right moment
it can be powerful. Ethical use only: true testimonials, case studies, etc., not false scarcity or fake
9

names. Also, the model can detect if buyer explicitly asks “Who else uses you?” and then feed the rep
the best reference to share.
• Reciprocity: If you give something, people feel obligated to reciprocate. In sales calls, this could
mean providing free insight or help. For example, educating the buyer on something useful
(unrelated to your product) or doing a quick audit for them may incline them to give you some
concession (time, candor, maybe the deal).
• Model action: Encourage giving value before asking. For instance, if the call starts cold, the model
might prompt the rep to share a useful industry insight or a small free analysis (“I took a look at your
website and noticed three areas your HVAC could save energy...”). This leverages reciprocity; the
buyer feels the rep is not just here to take. If an objection arises, sometimes giving a small
concession (or even just empathizing “I understand, you need to discuss with your partner – I have a
short guide on how to evaluate quotes I can send you both”) can build goodwill that the buyer
“repays” by engaging more.
• Confirmation Bias: Buyers tend to seek and favor information that confirms what they already
believe, and downplay info that contradicts it 40 . If a buyer believes “Solution X is probably the best
because it’s popular,” they’ll cherry-pick evidence. This is dangerous if they have misconceptions (like
undervaluing your approach).
• Model action: Identify the buyer’s core beliefs early by listening for clues (“We think the safest choice
is sticking with a well-known brand”). Then, strategically introduce information that reshapes their
belief without directly confronting. For example, ask questions that lead them to discover
exceptions (“You mentioned the well-known brand because reliability matters. Have you experienced
issues with them? … If reliability is key, would you explore an option that can demonstrably improve
uptime by 20%?”). The model should help the rep gently challenge false beliefs by offering data or
reframing in a question, rather than bluntly saying “You’re wrong.” This way the buyer can update
their own stance (self-persuasion). In other words, use the buyer’s existing values to align them with
a new perspective (Challenger’s “teach tailored to their driver” approach 41 11 ).
• Escalation of Commitment (Sunk Cost): If buyers have already invested a lot in a path (even a
failing one), they irrationally keep investing (classic sunk cost fallacy) 42 43 . For example, a
company might stick with a vendor because they’ve spent months implementing it, even if it’s not
working great, just because “we’ve come this far.”
• Model action: The model can equip the rep to tactfully address sunk cost fears. For instance, it might
suggest emphasizing a low-risk trial or intermediate step so the buyer doesn’t feel their past
investment is wasted (e.g. “You don’t have to rip everything out; what if we pilot this in one
department? That way your previous work isn’t lost, it’s just augmented.”). Also, highlight the future
costs of sticking to the wrong path vs the relatively smaller cost of adjusting course now 44 45 . The
model can detect language like “we’ve already put so much into X” and cue a maneuver to introduce
new information that justifies reconsidering (maybe an “Unconsidered need” that the current path
can’t address 36 ).
• Emotional vs. Logical Brain: Buyers often decide emotionally and justify logically. They might have
unspoken emotional drivers (fear of failure, desire for praise, personal convenience). A purely logical
10

pitch might miss the mark. For example, selling home security: logically it’s about cameras and locks,
emotionally it’s about family safety (fear of break-ins).
• Model action: Ensure messaging hits both emotional and logical notes. If a conversation is too stuck
on facts and figures, the model might encourage a story or question to evoke emotion (“How would
it feel to have peace of mind that...”). If a buyer is very emotional (angry about an issue), the model
should coach to acknowledge the emotion (label it) and show empathy first before logic. For
instance, with an angry customer, first validate: “It sounds like you’ve been really frustrated by these
delays, and I don’t blame you for feeling that way.” This diffuses the emotion (Chris Voss’s labeling
technique) 17 , then the rep can transition to solution mode. The AI should recognize emotional
spikes in sentiment analysis and prioritize empathy/rapport moves in those moments, whereas with
a very analytical buyer it might lean more on data and logic to satisfy their decision process (but still
pepper in social proof or risk-of-inaction to create urgency, because purely analytical can lead to no
decision if they overthink).
Conflict De-escalation and Emotional Regulation
In sales calls, conflicts or tense moments can arise (objections, complaints, misunderstandings). Handling
these gracefully is key.
De-escalation: If a buyer becomes angry or upset (perhaps about a past issue or feeling pressured), the
rep’s job is to calm the situation. Techniques from conflict resolution include: staying calm, active listening,
acknowledging feelings, apologizing if appropriate, and not interrupting. Importantly, no argument – you
can’t win an argument with a customer and still win the sale.
• Model guidance: Identify vocal cues of anger or frustration (raised voice, harsh words) or content like
“This is ridiculous…” and immediately alert the rep with a de-escalation mode. The model might
display: “Buyer is upset – do not push. Listen and acknowledge. Possible response: ‘I can hear that
you’re frustrated about X, and I’m really sorry about the trouble. Let’s see how we can make this right.’” The
model should essentially press the “empathy and accountability” button: advise the rep to apologize
sincerely if the company caused an issue, or at least empathize (“I understand why you’d feel that
way”). It might also suggest offering to fix the issue or taking a break (“Would you like a moment, or
is there someone else you’d like me to involve to help?”). The key is soothing the emotion first. Only
once the person feels heard should the rep gently steer back to problem-solving. The AI will coach
not to get defensive (“It’s not our fault” is a no-go) and not to match anger with anger.
Emotional regulation for the rep: Sometimes a buyer might say something that could offend or stress the
rep (like a personal dig: “You salespeople are all the same!”). An elite seller doesn’t snap back; they keep
professional. Our system can help an average rep by acting as a “co-pilot” that remains objective.
• Model guidance: In moments of provocation, a simple alert like “(Take a breath – maintain calm
authority)” could help. The model might even detect the rep’s own voice (if integrated) sounding
shaky or fast and prompt: “Slow down, speak calmly.” Post-call, if a conflict happened, the model
should coach how it was handled and how to improve (e.g. “When the buyer raised their voice about
the delay, you continued with the script. Consider pausing and acknowledging their frustration next
time.”).

“Frame” and Power Dynamics: We touched on frame control; here’s more. In conversation, there’s often a
subtle negotiation of status – who is leading, who is following. Buyers may try to assert dominance if they
perceive a power imbalance (sometimes to get a better deal, or just habit). Sellers must project an equal
business stature. This involves language (e.g., not overusing deferential phrases like “if it pleases you”),
and setting some boundaries (politely).
• Model guidance: The AI should detect overly deferential language by the rep – if a rep says “I’m sorry
to bother you… I promise I won’t take much time… (sounding meek)” the model can flag this as a
language anti-pattern. Also, if the buyer is steamrolling (e.g., interrupting frequently, ignoring
questions), the AI can suggest strategies to regain control: perhaps use the person’s name and ask a
direct question to interrupt their pattern, or recap and park their point (“I’ll absolutely address that –
could I clarify one thing first?” which is a way to take control of the agenda). Essentially, coach the
rep to lead the dance politely.
When Empathy Helps vs. When It Reduces Authority: Empathy is generally positive – understanding and
reflecting the buyer’s feelings builds trust. But there’s a nuance: overdoing empathy or doing it at the wrong
time can reduce the rep’s authority. For example, constantly saying “I’m so sorry” or overly aligning with the
buyer’s negative sentiment (“Yes, these prices are really high, I agree”) might undermine the rep’s position
or make them seem weak. The challenger sale research found that reps who were purely focused on
relationship comfort (always agreeing, never pushing) actually underperformed 46 3 . The highest
performers (Challengers) still have empathy, but they couple it with the boldness to challenge and lead.
• Guidance: The model should advise empathy particularly in emotional moments (e.g. when buyer
expresses a personal concern or frustration – then definitely show empathy). However, in moments
where the buyer might be testing or where a firm answer is needed, too much hedging or softening
can backfire. For instance, if a buyer says “I just don’t think you have the experience,” a pure
empathy response “I completely understand you don’t trust us at all” might reinforce their doubt.
Instead, a response might combine empathy with authority: “I understand experience is important –
you want to be in good hands. Our team has done XYZ (authority statement)…” In other words,
address the feeling but then lead with a solution or reassurance.
• The model might maintain a balance: encourage empathy statements but not self-diminishing ones.
Also caution against empathetic language that lowers status like “I’m sorry I am not explaining
well” (taking undue blame). Sometimes, to maintain authority, it’s better to express understanding
without self-blame: “I sense this isn’t coming across clearly – let’s go over it in a different way.” That’s
empathetic (acknowledges confusion) but still confident.
Ethical Persuasion: Underlying all of this, we emphasize persuasion ethics. The model will not encourage
deceit (like fake deadlines or false claims). Techniques like framing, anchoring, etc., can be used ethically by
sticking to truthful statements and real examples. The idea is to help buyers make the best decision
(which we believe is going with our client’s solution if it truly fits), not to trick them. Any psychological
technique recommended is aimed at clarifying value, reducing irrational fears, and building a healthy
relationship – not manipulation. We also ensure compliance (if a buyer says something indicating
vulnerability or legal issues, the system flags for human intervention).
By embedding these psychological principles, GoMotto will be adept at handling the human side of sales
calls. It will know when to apply pressure and when to alleviate it, when to appeal to logic vs emotion,
12

how to build trust and credibility from the outset, and how to navigate the biases that influence
buying decisions. Next, we will operationalize these ideas into a library of maneuvers – concrete strategic
moves that leverage these principles in specific situations.
3) Sales Playbook as a Library of “Maneuvers” (Not Scripts)
Rather than rigid scripts, GoMotto will operate with a dynamic library of maneuvers – strategic “plays” that
can be adapted and sequenced based on context. Think of a maneuver as a self-contained strategy to solve
a particular psychological or tactical challenge in the sale. Each maneuver has a purpose, triggers, and
method of execution, but is not a word-for-word script. It’s more like a dance move that can be performed in
various ways depending on the music of the conversation.
Below, we define key maneuvers. For each, we outline the Name, Intent (the psychological or strategic
problem it addresses), Best Triggers (signals or situations where it’s effective), Contraindications (when
not to use it), Risk Profile (what can go wrong if misused), Required Authority Level (does the rep need
high trust/credibility first?), Execution Sequence, and Example Lines for different buyer types.
This “playbook” will inform the Strategic layer: GoMotto will choose among these maneuvers in real time.
Crucially, multiple maneuvers can be valid in a given situation; our system will weigh them (Section 6
covers strategy selection). Also, maneuvers can be combined (e.g. an Authority Anchor followed by a
Reframe to Risk). The mantra: There is never one “correct” move—only better or worse fits for the frame,
pressure, engagement, and context at hand.
Strategic Maneuver Layer (Critical Concept)
Definition: A maneuver is a strategic action taken in response to the current context of the conversation,
aiming to improve the chance of a successful outcome. It’s more than a single sentence—it often involves a
short sequence of actions or dialogue turns. Each maneuver shifts something in the conversation: the level
of pressure, the perspective (frame), the amount of information flowing, the emotional climate, or the
buyer’s commitment level.
Think of the conversation as having dials (trust, urgency, clarity on needs, etc.). A maneuver is used to dial
something up or down. For example, the Pullback/Disqualification maneuver dials down pressure on the
buyer by showing you’re not desperate for the sale, which can paradoxically increase the buyer’s pursuit if
they are qualified. Another example, Reframe to Process, might dial down emotion by shifting to a neutral
planning discussion (if things got heated or stuck on one point).
We will now list a set of core maneuvers that our system should recognize and deploy. This list is not
exhaustive, but covers the minimum set we consider important:
Maneuver: Pullback / Disqualification
• Intent: Reduce perceived sales pressure and flip the dynamic so the buyer chases the value, by
showing that you (the seller) are okay with walking away. This solves problems like: buyer is resistant
or suspicious, thinks “the salesperson just wants to sell me anything,” or if the deal is stuck with non-
committal responses.
13

• Best Triggers: Buyer is expressing strong skepticism (“I don’t think this is for me”), pushing hard on
price early (“Unless you cut the price, forget it”), or generally disengaging (low responsiveness,
“maybe call me later”). Also when you sense you’re over-selling or the buyer has all the power in
frame. It’s often used after you’ve provided value or insight but the buyer still isn’t opening up – a
pullback can prompt them to reveal true objections.
• Contraindications: Do not use if the buyer has high interest or is ready to move forward (no need to
pull back then!). Avoid if the buyer hasn’t yet seen any value (pulling away too early might make
them think “Okay, bye”). Also, if the buyer is extremely passive or timid, a strong disqualification
might actually make them just agree to walk – you need some level of interest or ego in the buyer to
get the “pullback” effect. Not for trivial objections; best for core “go/no-go” hesitation.
• Risk Profile: Medium-High risk. If mis-timed or delivered without proper tone, the buyer could take
it as a genuine dismissal and end the call. It can offend if not done tactfully (“Maybe you’re not a fit”
can sound like “you’re not good enough”). Also, if the rep hasn’t established any authority or value,
pulling back can simply lose the sale. But when done right, it can dramatically change the power
dynamic in the seller’s favor.
• Required Authority Level:Moderate to high. Ideally, prior to a pullback, the rep should have done
something to establish credibility or good intent (e.g. shared an insight or at least demonstrated
they understand the buyer). Also requires the rep to sound confident when saying it. If a rep with
shaky voice says “maybe this isn’t a fit,” it might just come off as giving up. The rep needs enough
status so that their potential disengagement matters to the buyer.
• Execution Sequence: The proper sequence is typically (1) Validate → (2) Authority Anchor → (3)
Disqualify → (4) Silence:
• Validate the buyer’s perspective: Acknowledge their concern or stance so you don’t seem combative.
e.g. “I understand you have a tight budget and this is a big decision.” or “I get that you’ve used XYZ
for years and it’s hard to switch.”
• Authority Anchor (optional but powerful): Reinforce why you’re confident in your offering or your
customer fit criteria. e.g. “As a policy, we only take on clients when we’re sure we can deliver ROI,” or
“From what I’ve seen with hundreds of projects (establishing you have broad experience), ...”.
• Disqualify/Pullback statement: This is the core pullback: Politely suggest that maybe it’s not a fit,
giving the buyer an “out.” e.g. “...and it might turn out that we’re not the right solution for you.”
or more direct, “...and I honestly don’t know if we can help in your case; it might make sense to not
move forward.” This often follows the authority anchor: “We insist on only doing business when it
truly helps the client. If we’re not a fit, I’ll tell you. And it could be the case here.”
• Silence: After delivering the pullback, stop talking. This is crucial. Let the buyer respond. The silence
creates a void the buyer will often fill by either disagreeing (which means they start selling you on
why they do need help) or by revealing true concerns (“It’s not that I don’t want it, I’m just not
convinced about X”). If you break the silence too soon, you lose the effect.
• Example Lines (Variations by Buyer Type):

• Residential Homeowner: “I totally understand you need to discuss this with your spouse and you’re
worried about the cost. To be honest, I only want you to go forward if you both feel 100% confident.
It’s possible our solution isn’t the cheapest and maybe it isn’t right for you, and that’s okay.
[silence]”
• Small Business Owner: “You mentioned you’ve had bad experiences with marketing agencies. I can
see why you’d be cautious. You know, our agency turns down clients if we’re not sure we can
genuinely help – and we’ve walked away from deals where it wasn’t a win-win. This might be one of
those cases; it might not be a fit here, and that’s the last thing I want, to waste your time.
[silence]”
• Procurement Officer: “You’re focused on the numbers, and I respect that. We only engage if we can
meet strict ROI requirements. If our proposal can’t meet your criteria, we wouldn’t want you to

In each case, note the polite tone and the use of maybe or it might – it’s not an aggressive “This isn’t a fit,
bye,” it’s a subtle invitation for the buyer to either agree (rare, unless they truly were unqualified) or to say
“Wait, wait, I didn’t say that.” Many buyers, at this point, will backpedal or clarify – e.g. “Well, it’s not that we
don’t need it… it’s just…” – and now you have the real issue on the table or them re-engaging.
• What Can Go Wrong: If the buyer simply says “Yes, you’re probably right, bye.” That means either
they really weren’t a fit or the maneuver was misused (maybe they felt insulted or truly
uninterested). Also, some buyers might perceive it as a sales tactic and call it out (“Oh, is this the part
where you say maybe we shouldn’t do business? I’ve heard that before.”). In that case, authenticity is
key: the rep should genuinely be okay to walk away. It’s not a trick; we must be prepared to accept a
no. The model will monitor buyer’s tone after a pullback. If the buyer says “okay” in a relieved tone,
maybe they really want out – pushing further would hurt trust. So the system would then guide the
rep to professionally wrap up and leave the door open for future, rather than trying another heavy
maneuver.
Maneuver: Authority Anchor
• Intent: To bolster the rep’s credibility and authority in the eyes of the buyer, thus increasing the
buyer’s trust in recommendations. This maneuver addresses situations where the buyer might be
questioning, “Why should I listen to you?” or where the rep’s status needs a boost (for example, early
in a call or when facing a skeptical technical expert). It’s about subtly asserting expertise or
uniqueness without bragging.
• Best Triggers: Use when a buyer shows signs of doubt about your competency or track record (e.g.
asks “Have you done this before?” or “Why should we choose you over others?”). Also proactively at
the start of a call, especially in consultative selling, to set the tone (e.g. mention relevant
experience). Triggered if buyer mentions a competitor or seems to think you’re a commodity. Also if
buyer is in a high-status role (like a CEO or engineer) who might have a “show me you’re an expert”
attitude.
• Contraindications: Avoid when trust is already high and the buyer is convinced of your expertise
(no need to beat a dead horse). Don’t overdo it—multiple authority anchors can come off as
arrogance. Do not use right after you’ve made a mistake (first recover from the mistake humbly).
15

Also, if buyer is very relationship-focused and casual, a big authority drop might feel too formal;
blend it in naturally.
• Risk Profile: Low to medium risk. Usually it helps, but if done poorly, it can seem like boasting or
one-upmanship, which might turn off a buyer (especially if they pride themselves on being the
expert). Or it can come across as defensive if used in the wrong tone (“Actually, I’m a certified
expert…” in a defensive tone is bad). Need to execute with humility + confidence combo.
• Required Authority Level: This is about creating authority, so initially none is required, but the rep
must deliver it credibly. Facts need to check out; if you claim expertise and then can’t answer a basic
question, authority is lost. So use facts or references that can be backed up.
• Execution Sequence: Can be woven into conversation or delivered as a mini-monologue with data:
• Lead-in: Often starts in response to a buyer question or a natural point. Could also be pre-planned
in intro. For example, “You asked about our experience in healthcare...” or “Let me share something
from our work with 50 other HVAC projects...”
• Credibility statement: Provide a concise statement of your relevant experience, credentials, or
insight. Examples: “I’ve been doing solar installations for 12 years, and in that time I’ve seen ...” or
“Our team recently completed a project very similar to yours – actually 20% larger in scope.” Or data:
“We’ve analyzed over 500,000 sales calls with AI 47 , and we found ...” (Using a credible statistic can
position you as data-driven authority).
• Insight or Value add: Follow the credential with a piece of insight or something useful to the buyer.
Authority lands better if it’s not just “here’s why I’m great,” but rather “here’s something I know that
helps you.” E.g., “... and one pattern we noticed is that top performers did X 1 . So in your case, I
suspect doing Y might change the game.” Or “... which is why I suggest [some recommendation].”
This ties your authority to their benefit.
• (Optional) Evidence or Social proof: If possible, anchor it with evidence: “This method saved
another client 30% on energy bills” or “That’s documented in a case study I can share.” This can
overlap with social proof maneuver.
• Check-in (sometimes): You might gauge reaction or ask a question to keep it two-way. “Does that
align with what you’ve seen?” – This keeps authority from turning into a lecture and invites the buyer
to engage with your expertise rather than feel lectured.

• Home services context: “We’ve installed over 500 HVAC systems in homes just like yours in the Phoenix

• B2B software: “As a quick background, our team at GoMotto has analyzed over half a million sales
conversations with AI to understand what top reps do differently 48 . One insight we discovered is

• Agency or consultancy: “I’ve personally led digital marketing strategy for Fortune 500 brands and

spend can increase ROI if it’s refocused on the highest-performing channels. (Here the authority:
Fortune 500 experience; insight: counterintuitive tip indicating deep expertise.)”
• What Can Go Wrong: If the buyer doesn’t care about credentials (e.g. they just want the bottom

and spew an irrelevant credential (like talking Fortune 500 to a small local business owner – could
intimidate or alienate them), that’s a mismatch. The model will advise using relevant authority
markers for the persona (e.g., homeowners care that you’ve done many local installs, not that you
have a PhD in engineering). We should avoid too much jargon in authority statements unless talking
to a technical buyer. Additionally, an authority anchor must always be truthful; any exaggeration can
backfire if found out. The system should source actual data points or customer counts from CRM to
feed the rep accurate info (“we’ve serviced X customers in your industry”).
Maneuver: Curiosity Loop / Diagnosis
• Intent: To engage the buyer by triggering their curiosity and self-discovery, and to thoroughly
diagnose their situation. This is often a series of tailored questions that get the buyer to open up
about their needs, pain points, and goals – but framed in a way that also intrigues them or makes
them think in new ways. It’s based on the idea that people are more persuaded when they voice the
problem/implication rather than just hearing it from the rep. This maneuver addresses scenarios
where the buyer is not fully revealing information or doesn’t yet feel the pain/urgency of their
problem.
• Best Triggers: Early discovery phase of a call, or when hitting a surface-level need but suspect
deeper issues. Also when buyer is somewhat engaged but maybe too passive – good questioning can
draw them out. Or if a buyer seems indifferent, a provocative question can spark interest. NEPQ
(Neuro-Emotional Persuasion Questions) style selling is all about these sequences: problem
awareness → consequence awareness → solution awareness questions. Use when you have time to
explore (maybe not when buyer says “I have 5 minutes”). Also if buyer makes a statement that begs
for exploration (“Our process is a mess”) – jump into diagnosis.
• Contraindications: If the buyer is impatient or time-crunched, a long question loop can annoy
them. If they explicitly say, “I don’t have time for twenty questions, just tell me what you’ve got,” you
might need a different strategy (maybe a quick summary then ask a pointed question). Also, avoid
overly broad or cliché questions (“What keeps you up at night?”) unless you can do it in a fresh way –
savvy buyers might roll their eyes. Don’t interrogate – it’s a conversation, not an interview. If buyer
already articulated clear needs, don’t belabor it with too many questions – instead confirm and move
forward.
• Risk Profile: Low to medium. Asking questions generally reduces risk of talking past the customer,
but if done poorly (like a checklist or irrelevant questions), it wastes time and frustrates. Also, a risk is
not getting to value quickly enough if you ask too many low-impact questions. The model should
prioritize high-impact questions and monitor buyer engagement (if answers get short, pivot away
from questioning).
17

• Required Authority Level: Low to moderate. You can ask questions even with no authority
established (it’s how you earn it). However, certain deeper or sensitive questions might require some
rapport first. For example, you can’t immediately ask “What’s your budget?” without building some
trust – that could be too direct early. The model can arrange question order to build up comfort
(start with easier context questions, then problem, then implications, etc., akin to SPIN Selling flow).
• Execution Sequence: Often follows a funnel: open broad → go deeper → highlight implications
→ loop back to value:
• Contextual Questions (Situation): Start with understanding context (if not known). “How are you
currently handling X?” or “What’s your setup for Y today?” (SPIN’s Situation questions – but careful,
minimize these because too many situational questions bore buyers 50 . Only ask what you don’t
know and is relevant).
• Problem Questions: Explore pain points: “What are the biggest challenges you face with that?” or
“You mentioned delays – why do you think that’s happening?” These get the buyer to articulate pain.
• Curiosity Spark (if needed): Insert an unexpected question or observation that makes them think.
Example: “If you could wave a magic wand, what would ideal look like?” or “I’m curious, why not just
stay with the current system forever?” (asked sincerely, not sarcastically). Sometimes flipping
perspective works: “It sounds like things are okay – is there a reason you’re even considering a
change now?” Asking this can provoke them to justify the change (they sell themselves). Use only if
they haven’t expressed a clear reason yet.
• Implication Questions: Here’s NEPQ/ SPIN magic – get them to state consequences. “What happens
if you do nothing about this for another year?” or “How do these delays impact your bottom line or
your team’s workload?” or “Have you calculated what the downtime costs in lost revenue?” These
questions make the buyer quantify or feel the pain more 29 51 . If they haven’t thought about it, it
plants a seed of urgency.
• Solution / Value Questions: Finally, ask about desired outcomes or value of solving it: “If you could
solve this, what would it mean for the business? Would it free you up to do X?” This gets them to
articulate the payoff they want, which you can later link to your solution.
• Loop and Label: Throughout, use active listening. Summarize: “So, if I heard right, the main issue is
A, which causes B. And if fixed, you’d gain C. Is that about right?” This summary both shows you
listened and sometimes clarifies thinking for the buyer. It might also surface corrections or
additional points.
• Transition with Curiosity: If done well, by this point the buyer is more aware of their needs and
possibly curious about how to fix it. Sometimes they’ll ask, “So, how would you tackle this?” If not,
you can transition: “That’s really insightful. It sounds like solving X could [mention benefit they care
about]. I’m thinking if there’s a way to get you that result…” This phrasing piques curiosity that
you might have a solution. Essentially, you’ve built a gap that they want to fill.
• Example Lines:
• Rep: “Walk me through your current process for scheduling service calls.” (Context)
◦ Buyer: “We use an online form, then someone calls back… it’s kind of manual.”
• Rep: “Got it. What’s the biggest frustration you have with how that works now?” (Problem)
◦ Buyer: “We sometimes lose track of requests, and customers get mad about slow replies.”

• Rep: “I see. How often does that happen, and what does it lead to when it does?” (Implication)
◦ Buyer: “Maybe a few times a month, and we’ve definitely lost some business or gotten bad
reviews from it.”
• Rep: “Those missed requests, any idea how much in sales that might be costing, say in a
month?” (Quantify Implication)
◦ Buyer: “Hard to say, maybe a couple jobs, which could be a few thousand dollars.”
• Rep: “A few thousand a month, that adds up. And beyond the money, I imagine it impacts your
reputation too.” (Further implication, labeling the emotional aspect like reputation risk)
◦ Buyer: “Yeah, it does. We pride ourselves on service, so it’s damaging.”
• Rep: “Understood. So if you could wave a magic wand, those requests would never slip through and

◦ Buyer: “Exactly, that would be ideal.”
• Rep: (Summarizing) “So, missing customer inquiries is causing lost revenue and some reputation hit.

◦ Buyer: “Yes, that’s right.”
• Rep: “Thanks for that context. It gets me thinking about some solutions that might ensure exactly
that outcome for you... (transition into pitching how we solve it)”
In that dialogue, the rep used curiosity (“wave a magic wand”), implication (lost revenue, reputation), and
got the buyer to state the pain and ideal. The buyer is now more motivated to hear a solution.
• What Can Go Wrong: Pitfalls include:
• Interrogation mode: firing questions one after another without reacting to answers. The model will
ensure after a major answer, the rep either reflects (“That makes sense”) or uses the info before
jumping to the next question.
• The buyer might say “I don’t know” or be guarded. In such cases, the model can suggest alternate
approaches: maybe share a hypothesis or third-party example to provoke a response (“Some
companies in your shoes struggle with X – do you see that too?”).
• Time management: in a short call, maybe you can’t fully flesh out implication questions; the model
might prioritize which question yields most insight quickly (e.g. ask about their top pain and one
implication, then move on).
• If buyer has already done a formal needs assessment, be careful not to ask things they think you
should know (maybe glean from CRM).
This Curiosity/Diagnosis maneuver corresponds heavily to frameworks like SPIN (Situation, Problem,
Implication, Need-Payoff) 52 and NEPQ (which emphasizes emotional questions). Our AI will have a bank of
powerful questions and logic on when to use them, making the rep appear thoughtful and genuinely
interested (which they should be!).
Maneuver: Reframe to Process
• Intent: Shift a difficult or unproductive conversation into a focus on the process or next steps,
effectively changing the frame of discussion. This is useful to diffuse tension, avoid getting stuck on
one issue, or to regain control by moving into planning mode. It addresses scenarios where, for
example, the buyer is hyper-fixated on a detail (price, a minor feature, etc.) or where an objection
can’t be resolved immediately – instead of arguing, the rep reframes to talk about how a decision
19

would be made or what the overall timeline looks like. It can also be used to gracefully handle an
objection by scheduling it into the process (“Let’s do X, then we’ll address that concern as part of Y”).
• Best Triggers: Buyer repeatedly pressing on something you can’t solve right now (like “I need to see
the contract” too early, or “I’m worried about implementation complexity” before they’ve seen a
demo). Or if conversation is going in circles. Also, when a call is running out of time, reframing to
process is a way to secure a commitment for next step instead of trying to resolve everything now.
It’s frequently used in complex sales to keep momentum: e.g., if buyer says “I have to talk to my
team,” you reframe to schedule that meeting rather than debate the idea further in absence of the
team. It’s a way to park a debate and move forward.
• Contraindications: Don’t use if the buyer’s concerns are critical and emotional – in those cases,
address feelings first (empathy) before process. Also, not for when buyer is ready to sign now (then
just close!). If overused, the buyer might feel you’re evading their questions. So ensure any major
questions get at least acknowledged and slated for a specific future action, not just ignored.
• Risk Profile: Low if done transparently. It can defuse conflict. The risk is if the buyer feels
stonewalled or handled – like you’re just kicking the can. To mitigate, be clear that you’re reframing
for their benefit (“so we can give you the right info at the right time, let’s...”). Also risk: if you promise
to address something later and then don’t, trust is lost. So process commitments must be kept.
• Required Authority Level: Low. Even a junior rep can say “Can we step back and outline next steps?”
That said, having a slight authority or trust helps so they follow your lead. But generally, focusing on
process is neutral ground and often welcome (“this person is organized”).
• Execution Sequence:
• Acknowledge Current Thread: Don’t ignore what they said; briefly validate or note it. “Absolutely,
pricing is important.”
• Propose shifting to process context: e.g. “Let’s do this: [outline a process].” For instance, “I think it
might help to step back and map out the decision process so we make sure we cover everything you
need.”
• Outline Process Clearly: Provide a structure that addresses their issue at a later stage. “Typically,
clients want to see the detailed implementation plan – we usually cover that in a dedicated meeting
with our tech team. How about we set that up? In the meantime, we’ll… [whatever next step].” Or
“Why don’t we do this: I’ll send you a detailed proposal with pricing break-down (addressing their
concern in a deliverable) and we schedule a review call next Tuesday to go over it. Does that work?”
• Gain Agreement: Make sure they’re on board with this new direction. If they say yes, you’ve
successfully reframed. If they resist (“No, I need to know now”), then you know this is a must-
address-now issue, and you pivot to another approach (maybe tackling it head-on or pulling in
someone).
• Follow through: If you set a plan, summarize it: “Great, so to recap, I’ll do X, we’ll meet Y, and then
decide on Z.” This gives them comfort that there is a method.
• Example Lines:
20

• Buyer: “I need to know the price right now or I can’t continue.”
• Rep (reframe): “Understood, pricing is top of mind. Can we outline the process first? What I’d like
to do is go through exactly what you’d be getting, then I can provide a detailed quote. In fact, let’s
pencil a meeting specifically for pricing review with all stakeholders – say Thursday? That way you
have the full context. Does that sound fair?”
◦ (Here rep acknowledges, then suggests a process: understand needs -> provide detailed
quote in a pricing meeting, rather than blurting out half-baked price).
• Buyer: “I want to try the product before we talk any further.”
• Rep: “Makes sense – a trial is a great idea. Tell you what, let’s map out a quick plan: we do a two-
week trial where you can use it, and we schedule a 30-minute check-in mid-trial to ensure it’s going
well. If it’s not solving your problem, we part ways. If it is, then we discuss the purchase. How does
that approach sound?”
◦ (Turned a demand into a structured process, reducing their fear and giving them control in a
scheduled way.)
• Buyer: (angry about an issue) “Your installation takes too long compared to others!”
• Rep (after apologizing): “I want to address that fully. What I suggest is this: let’s have my technical
lead join our next call to walk you through the timeline and how we manage it. We can incorporate
any requirements you have. For now, maybe we can discuss what date you’d ideally want it done by,
so we can bring that to the planning discussion?”
◦ (The rep defers detailed argument by scheduling tech discussion, but still engages by asking
for the buyer’s ideal – which is part of process planning.)
• What Can Go Wrong: If the buyer feels you’re not listening or stalling. We must ensure the
acknowledgement is sincere and specific: e.g. “I know you have a concern about X.” If you jump to
process without that, they may just repeat the concern louder. Also, the new process suggestion
must actually address their concern eventually; otherwise it’s a dodge. The model should suggest
realistic next steps (if a buyer wants a trial, don’t avoid it – maybe offering a structured trial is the
process). Another risk: They might say “No, I want to resolve this now.” In that case, plan B: the
model might escalate to a different maneuver or provide the info if possible. But often just the act of
organizing the discussion can calm people down.
How it changes power/trust/pressure: Reframing to process often reduces immediate pressure (we’re
not solving it right this second, we have a plan) and can increase trust by showing you’re organized and not
pushy. It can also subtly regain frame control: if the buyer was driving the convo into a corner, you step up
as a facilitator of the process, putting you in a leadership role. It signals professionalism.
Maneuver: Reframe to Risk (Making the Problem the “Enemy”)
• Intent: To change the conversation’s perspective by positioning the problem or status quo as the
common enemy, rather than any contention between buyer and seller. This is a form of reframing
21

where you highlight the risks of not solving the problem (which aligns with loss aversion) and
essentially get the buyer and you on the same side, tackling the problem. It’s related to Challenger’s
method of making customers see their current approach as risky. The psychological goal is to instill
urgency and a shared mission.
• Best Triggers: When a buyer is complacent or lacking urgency (“We’re not sure if we need to act
now”), or when they say things like “We’re doing okay as is.” Also if a deal is stalling (“call me in 6
months”). Another trigger: when a buyer focuses only on cost of solution, you might need to reframe
to the cost of the problem or risk of inaction. Use also when multiple stakeholders are involved
and you need a rallying point (“If this doesn’t get fixed, all departments suffer X risk”).
• Contraindications: Don’t overuse fear – it must be credible and not FUD (fear, uncertainty, doubt)
for its own sake. If the buyer already is very aware of the risk or even anxious, piling on might stress
them; you then might need to reassure instead. Also, if the relationship is new, be careful about
coming in heavy with “big risk” statements – provide evidence, not just “the sky will fall.” And
ethically, ensure the risk is real (no exaggeration).
• Risk Profile: Low if factual, high if baseless. Raising real risks the buyer hasn’t considered can
position you as a helpful advisor (e.g. “Have you thought about what happens if...” which they
appreciate). But if they sense you’re just trying to scare them into buying, trust can erode. It requires
finesse and supporting data or anecdotes.
• Required Authority Level: Moderate. If you’re going to talk about big risks in their business, it helps
if they believe you know what you’re talking about (or you cite credible sources). Often this maneuver
is done after some discovery, so you have ammo. If you’re a newbie, perhaps quote industry
research (“According to a study, 60% of small businesses don’t recover after a major data loss” – if
selling backups). That borrowed authority can suffice.
• Execution Sequence:
• Identify a potential risk in their status quo or problem. This comes from discovery: e.g., they have
no backup generator in a hospital (huge risk), or they rely on one supplier, or their roof has known
issues but they’re delaying.
• Ask or Highlight that risk: “Have you considered what would happen if [worst case scenario]?” or “I’d
be remiss if I didn’t point out a risk I see...”. The tone should be concerned, collaborative, not gleeful
or pushy.
• Quantify or give example: “For instance, if that generator fails, operations stop – like what
happened to Hospital X last year 53 54 .” or “Your data shows you’re losing 5% of revenue each
month from churn – over a year that’s 60%, which is massive.”
• Project the future if unresolved: “Continuing without [solution], this could lead to [negative
outcome].” Make it vivid but realistic. If possible, use their own data (from implication questions or
case studies).
• Present solving as a safe haven: Then shift: “On the flip side, addressing this now means you avoid
that risk and actually [positive outcome].” Essentially: problem is enemy, our solution is the shield or
sword to defeat it.
22

• Invite dialogue: “How does that align with what you’ve seen?” or “Is that a risk you’re comfortable
with, or do you think it’s something that needs attention sooner than later?” This engages them to
either agree (thus internalizing urgency) or express doubt (which you can then handle with
evidence).
• Example Lines:
• “I know budget is tight and it’s tempting to postpone the roof replacement. The risk I have to
mention is that the leaks you have could lead to mold – I’ve seen situations where a small leak, left
for a year, forced a family out of their home for a full remediation. That cost 3x more than a proactive
fix. I’d hate to see that happen to you.” (Here, the rep frames the leak/mold as the mutual enemy
and themselves as the advisor trying to save the homeowner from it.)
• “You’re currently not monitoring your network for breaches in real-time. The scary truth is that
SMEs like yours are increasingly targeted – 40% experience a hack and many don’t realize until
months later. The downtime and legal costs can be crippling 29 55 . My goal is to ensure you’re
not one of those statistics by implementing early warning systems.” (Frame: hackers are enemy,
not buying our security has risk, I’m here to help prevent that. The use of a stat makes it credible.)
• In a group meeting scenario: “Each department has its way of doing things, which works day-to-day.
My concern is that if this continues without a unified system, you might hit a wall where nothing
scales and customers feel the chaos. We saw this at Company Y – they waited too long and ended up
in crisis, losing a big client due to internal miscommunication. We definitely want to avoid that
fate here. Let’s talk about how you can gradually unify processes before it becomes urgent.” (Using
a story of a company who didn’t act as the cautionary tale – making the status quo the villain.)
• What Can Go Wrong: If the buyer thinks “They’re just trying to scare me.” The remedy: always back
it with something (data, story, or their own earlier admission). Also, be careful not to insult their
current operations too harshly or sound like you’re rooting for failure. The tone is “I’m genuinely
concerned for you.” Also, ensure the risk is something your solution actually addresses. You don’t
want to raise a risk outside your scope (that’d be manipulative and not solvable by you). Another
issue: some buyers might downplay risk (“that won’t happen to us”). If they do, maybe pivot to
another point or accept they own that risk mentally (don’t beat a dead horse). The model can note
buyer responses and not keep harping if no effect.
This maneuver changes power by shifting focus off any seller vs buyer disagreement to both vs problem. It
increases urgency (pressure on the status quo, not on the buyer personally). It builds trust if done right
because you’re looking out for them – though it’s a fine line, hence must be delivered with credible insight,
not just “doom and gloom.”
Maneuver: “Enemy” Framing (Allies vs. Competitor or Objection)
(Ethical Version)
• Intent: Align the buyer and seller on the same side by introducing a figurative “enemy” or external
opponent that you together are against. This is related to frame control: instead of buyer vs. seller
negotiating, it’s buyer + seller vs. a common challenge. “Enemies” can be the problem (like above), a
competitor (careful ethically), or the status quo. Another flavor is to frame your solution and the
23

buyer as the heroes against a villain (storytelling technique). Done ethically, it can create
camaraderie and team mentality.
• Best Triggers: When there’s a competitor in play (frame it not as us vs them in a petty way, but like,
e.g., “old way vs new way” where competitor = old way). Or when buyer is skeptical, you can frame
skepticism itself as common sense and you join them in being skeptical about bad solutions
(implying other vendors might be pushing those). Also used in negotiation: e.g., if procurement is
pushing for a discount, position “my boss/approval process” as the mutual enemy causing delays,
not you and the buyer fighting (works only in some settings – must be careful to not lie). Another
scenario: in discovery, aligning with buyer’s dislike of something (like they hate paperwork – you join
in “we built our product because we too hate paperwork – it’s the enemy”).
• Contraindications: Don’t use to badmouth a competitor directly with falsehoods or too negatively –
ethical line. If buyer loves competitor X, framing them as enemy can backfire. Instead, maybe frame
the competitor’s approach or limitation as an impersonal foe (e.g., “manual processes” rather than
“Company X sucks”). Also, if the buyer is conflict-averse or very professional, they may not like overt
“us vs them” talk. Use subtlety.
• Risk Profile: Medium. If the buyer agrees and feels that camaraderie, great. If they find it
manipulative or unprofessional (like openly bashing competitor or scapegoating), it harms trust.
Must be done with finesse and often humor or narrative.
• Required Authority Level: Low to moderate. Often you can create an alliance even early if you find
something they care about and position that as our joint mission. But having rapport helps so it
doesn’t seem random.
• Execution Sequence:
• Identify the enemy relevant to the buyer. It could be an inefficiency, a big box competitor, a
challenge in their industry, etc.
• Express empathy or shared stance: “Like you, we can’t stand how ...” or “We’ve seen so many
companies suffer at the hands of ... and we’re determined to stop it.”
• Include buyer in the crusade: “What if together we could eliminate [enemy] for your company?
That’s essentially our goal.” Or even playful: “Think of us as your secret weapon against [enemy].”
• Substantiate (if needed): Show how your solution specifically overcomes that enemy. E.g.
“Paperwork is the bane of both our lives – our software automates 90% of it, freeing you from that
villain.”
• Check reaction: If they lean in or chuckle, good. If they seem unimpressed, maybe drop the
metaphor and just stick to facts.
• Example Lines:
• Selling against incumbent solution: “We all know that the real enemy is downtime. When your
system goes offline, it doesn’t matter whose name is on the box – you’re losing money every minute.
Our entire approach is about minimizing that downtime. So it’s really us together against
downtime, not you vs. us vs. other vendors.” (This frames the metric downtime as enemy,
24

positioning you as ally. The competitor’s weakness might be more downtime, implied but not
named.)
• Against a giant competitor (when you’re a smaller player): “Look, I know BigCorp might be the safer
choice on paper. But frankly, BigCorp doesn’t prioritize smaller clients – their enemy is any
customization or special attention. We’ve won business because we fight that attitude – we treat
your success as our success. The enemy is being a ‘small fish in a big pond.’ We make sure that
doesn’t happen to you.” (Here we framed impersonal enemy: being ignored by large vendors.)
• In a friendly rapport setting: “I get the sense you really dislike manual data entry. Believe me, we do
too – around here we call spreadsheets the Devil’s Grid (ha ha). We basically started this service to
kill that devil. So if we work together, our job is to banish Excel to the underworld and get you
automated.” (Light-hearted enemy framing, using humor, if appropriate to the buyer’s personality.)
• What Can Go Wrong: If buyer doesn’t resonate with the enemy you chose. Maybe they don’t see it
as a big deal or they have no animosity. In that case, you haven’t found the right rallying point. No
harm done if it’s mild, but if you push an enemy concept and they disagree (“Actually I have no
problem with spreadsheets”), then you adapt – maybe drop that angle. Also, cultural fit matters:
some very formal environments might not like “enemy” language or joking. Then do it more subtly:
“We see ourselves as helping clients overcome [challenge].” That’s a softer framing.
This maneuver can strongly increase engagement and alignment if successful: buyer feels the rep gets
them and is on their side. It can also differentiate your approach (especially against competitors) without
directly smearing them – you focus on problem vs. painting competitor as villain directly. It increases trust
because the buyer feels you have a common goal, not just trying to sell for selfish reasons.
Maneuver: Price Anchoring & Scope Control
• Intent: To manage price discussions strategically by setting anchors (reference points) and
controlling the scope of what’s being priced, thus maintaining value perception and avoiding
unnecessary discounting. Essentially: ensure the first number the buyer hears works in your favor
(either by you anchoring high on value/cost of problem, or by carefully responding to their anchor),
and if price is an issue, adjust what’s included (scope) rather than just the number. This solves
scenarios like: buyer asking for a discount, sticker shock, or early price questions that can derail
value talk.
• Best Triggers: Buyer asks “How much is this going to cost?” especially early on. Buyer reacts
negatively to initial price quote (“That’s higher than I thought!”). Procurement negotiations where
they try to push price down. Also when offering multiple options (you anchor with a high-end option
first). Another trigger: when you sense they have an unrealistic low expectation – good to anchor
reality by referencing typical investments. Also if buyer tries to compare to cheaper alternatives, you
may anchor by reframing total cost of ownership etc.
• Contraindications: If the buyer is already satisfied with the price or not price-sensitive (some
explicitly say “price is fine, more concerned about X”), don’t belabor anchoring high. If overused, can
come off as manipulative (so we anchor subtly, often by focusing on value metrics). Also, if it’s a
fixed-price market (like retail), anchoring is less relevant beyond highlighting value. Don’t anchor
25

with irrelevant numbers (like saying “it’s not $1 million, it’s $100k” if $1M was never in their mind, you
might introduce undue shock).
• Risk Profile: Medium. A poorly done anchor can scare a buyer off (“He said some companies spend
$1M, we can’t afford that at all!”). The key is to anchor with context that doesn’t directly apply to
them but sets a high ceiling, or to anchor to the problem cost. If you mis-estimate their budget in
anchoring talk, you could lose credibility. Scope control (removing features to reduce price) has risk
of them feeling they lose value – must frame as optimizing to what they need.
• Required Authority Level: Some trust helps. If you start a call, “Our product is $100k and that’s a
bargain because problem costs $1M” with no rapport, it can be off-putting. Better to establish some
value before heavy anchoring.
• Execution Sequence (Anchoring):
• Pre-empt or respond to price question by anchoring to a larger value: For example, if early, “We’ll
get to price – it does depend on the package. For context, some companies spend [high number] on
solutions like this because the problem can cost even more. Let’s see what you actually need.” This
plants a big number without quoting their price yet, making your eventual price seem reasonable.
• Anchor to Problem Value: “You shared that downtime costs ~$10k/hour. In a year that risk is $X00k.
Our solution is a fraction of that.” (So if product is $50k, and risk was $500k, you anchored at $500k).
• Offer a Range with High Anchor: If forced to give a number, give a range starting high: “Depending
on configuration, it can range from $50k up to $150k or more for enterprise setups. Smaller teams
usually invest around $60-70k.” The $150k is an anchor that makes $70k sound smaller.
• Watch reaction: See if buyer flinches or not. If not, you might even have more budget room. If yes,
follow with scope control or justification.
• Execution Sequence (Scope Control): (Often following a price objection)
• Acknowledge sticker shock: “I hear the price is higher than expected.”
• Emphasize value again: “That includes A, B, and C which ensure [desired outcome].”
• Offer scope adjustment instead of discount: “If budget is a concern, we can explore adjusting the
scope. Perhaps start with X and add Y later. That would bring the investment down to [lower
number].” This shifts the talk to trading off features or phases, not arbitrarily cutting price. It frames as
“there’s a cheaper version, but it has less – your call.”
• Make them choose: “Would you prefer we remove the premium support and lower the cost, or is
the full package important to you?” This often makes them reconsider dropping value and maybe
they find budget or at least feel in control.
• If discounting, get something in return: If absolutely needed, treat discount as a scope change or
get something (like longer contract, case study permission). The model can suggest this: “Offer 5%
off if they can sign by end of quarter (urgency) or if they agree to be reference.”
• Example Lines:
26

• Early anchor: “Just to set expectations, solutions in this space can run anywhere from a few thousand
to hundreds of thousands annually for large enterprises 56 57 . It really depends on scope. We’ll
make sure to tailor something that fits your needs and budget.” (If their mental budget was, say,
$1k, hearing “hundreds of thousands” resets them; then you say you’ll tailor, so they feel safe.)
• Problem cost anchor: “You mentioned one truck roll costs you ~$500. Last year you had 200 repeat
visits – that’s $100k lost. Our service costs about half of that, and aims to eliminate those repeat
visits. So it pays for itself twofold.”
• Price objection scope response: “Okay, we’re at $50k and you hoped for $40k. Understood. One way:
we included on-site training in that quote. If you handle training internally with our materials, we
could remove that cost and come closer to $40k. Or we could consider a smaller user count to start. I
wouldn’t want to remove any core feature you need – but let’s see what is optional for now.” (This
shows flexibility without immediately saying “I’ll discount 20%.” It invites them to consider what they
value most.)
• Negotiation trade: “If price is the only thing holding us back, we have some flexibility. For example, if
you can commit to a 3-year term, I can secure a more favorable rate for you, since that guarantees
our partnership long-term.” (Here you anchor by term, not chopping price for nothing.)
• What Can Go Wrong: If the buyer just wants a straight answer and you dance around too much,
they’ll get annoyed (“Why can’t you just tell me the price?!”). So the model should gauge how
insistent they are and possibly just give a number after one anchoring attempt. Also, giving too high
an anchor could scare truly small-budget clients away prematurely (“hundreds of thousands??” and
they mentally exit). So segment by customer type – e.g., don’t mention enterprise scales to a mom-
and-pop business. Use relative anchors (maybe anchor to what they might waste in status quo, or
mention a high-end competitor’s price if known to be higher than yours).
Another risk: if scope reduction is offered, buyer might think “oh, so you were overcharging for stuff I don’t
need.” Mitigate by framing it as phases or optional add-ons, not fluff. Always reinforce that the full package
is worth it but you can start lean.
This maneuver, if done right, protects margin while respecting the buyer’s need. It maintains a
collaborative tone on pricing rather than adversarial haggling. It also shows you’re thoughtful about their
ROI. GoMotto will have logic for when to anchor, what figures to use (maybe from similar customers or
known ROI metrics), and how to propose scope changes systematically.
Maneuver: Strategic Silence / Pause Tactics
• Intent: Use silence as a tool to encourage the buyer to speak more, especially after a difficult
question or statement, and to signal confidence. Silence can apply slight pressure on the buyer to fill
the gap, often leading them to reveal information or soften their stance. It also shows you’re not
overeager or rattled. This maneuver complements others (like Pullback or after asking a pointed
question).
• Best Triggers: Right after posing a strong question (e.g. “Is this investment something you’re
prepared to make to solve the problem?” then silence). After delivering a challenging statement (like
the disqualification line). Also if a buyer goes quiet considering something – resist the urge to jump
in too quickly. During negotiation: when a buyer gives an objection or condition, sometimes just
27

pausing makes them elaborate or even backtrack. Anytime you feel talking more would weaken your
position, use silence instead.
• Contraindications: If the buyer is truly stuck or shy, too much silence can make it awkward. Also
with certain personalities or cultures, silence might be misinterpreted. If on phone and silence goes
too long, they might think call dropped. So be mindful – normal pause is a few seconds, maybe up to
5-6 seconds in a tough negotiation, not 30 seconds. Also not great if the question was unclear – then
silence just confuses. Use when you know they heard you clearly and it’s on them to respond.
• Risk Profile: Low risk if done in moderation. The main risk is the rep gets uncomfortable and breaks
it themselves (which defeats the tactic). The model can actually have a timer or cue “hold for 3
seconds more.” Another risk: Buyer might say “Hello? You still there?” if not managed, but that rarely
happens if they know you’re expecting them to answer. If they do, you can simply say “Yes, I’m just
giving you a moment to think.”
• Required Authority Level: None explicitly, but when used by confident reps it works best. If a rep is
clearly new and just goes silent, it could feel like they don’t know what to say. So pair silence with
confident body language (if on video, maintain eye contact or a thoughtful expression). The AI might
even coach: “nod and stay quiet” after a disqualifier line.
• Execution Sequence:
• Ask or state, then shut up: After delivering whatever it is (question, statement), consciously mute
yourself (in mind). Count if needed.
• Non-verbal cues (if applicable): If on video or in person, maintain a neutral or attentive look.
Maybe slight nod as if awaiting response. If on phone, obviously they can’t see – just hold the line.
• Resist interrupting: Let the buyer be the next to talk. If they ask something back, answer if it’s on
topic, otherwise re-pose question and go silent again.
• Break silence if absolutely necessary: If it drags and you sense confusion, you can clarify or move
on, but try to avoid rescuing them too quickly.
• Example:
• (During discovery) Rep: “What happens if you don’t fix this issue in the next six months?”
◦ Buyer: “Um... I’m not sure. We might continue as is, I guess.”
◦ Rep: silent, just listening intently
◦ Buyer: “… Well, actually, it could become a bigger problem in peak season. We’d probably
have unhappy customers.”
◦ (The silent pause after “not sure” encouraged them to think deeper and continue.)
• (Handling an objection) Buyer: “Your price is too high.”
◦ Rep: looks at buyer, neutral expression, says nothing immediately
◦ Buyer (after a couple seconds): “I mean, it’s more than competitor X. Why are you higher?”
◦ (Now the rep got the real issue – competitor comparison – thanks to silence. If rep had
jumped in with discount or justification immediately, buyer might not have elaborated.)
28

• (After proposal) Rep: “To proceed, the investment is $50,000. [quiet].”
◦ Buyer: “… Hmm.” [quiet thinking]
◦ Rep: still silent, comfortable
◦ Buyer: “We did budget around 50, so it might work… but is that including support?”
◦ (They basically convinced themselves it’s in range. If rep had kept talking, they might have
accidentally down-sold or offered something. Silence let the buyer process the number.)
• What Can Go Wrong: The rep might break silence too soon – an AI coach can literally have a
reminder: “Don’t rush to fill silence – let them speak.” Another risk is if the buyer is also a master of
silence – then you get a standoff. If that happens and it’s really long (like both are quiet 10+
seconds), the rep can gently re-engage: “I’m sensing this is something you’re thinking over. Take your
time – I’d love to hear your thoughts.” (So you acknowledge the silence rather than just babbling out
of nervousness.)
In summary, silence is a subtle but powerful maneuver in the arsenal, used in conjunction with others
(especially pullback, tough questions, negotiation closes). It often leads to the buyer revealing more
information or making the next move, which is ideal.
Maneuver: Labeling and Mirroring
• Intent: Borrowed from FBI negotiator techniques (Chris Voss), Labeling is naming the emotion or
concern the buyer seems to have, and Mirroring is repeating the last few words or key word they
said. Both are designed to make the buyer feel heard and encourage them to elaborate 17 15 . This
maneuver solves cases where the buyer is either emotional or not fully explaining something.
Labeling defuses negative emotions and shows empathy; mirroring encourages the buyer to keep
talking without the rep needing to come up with a new question.
• Best Triggers: Buyer expresses a strong emotion or tone (frustration, hesitation, excitement even).
For example, buyer sighs and says “This is just so complicated.” Label that: “It seems like you’re
feeling overwhelmed with the complexity.” Or buyer says a phrase that is loaded or unclear: “We’re
just not ready.” Mirror: “Not ready?” (in a gentle questioning tone) to prompt them to explain. Also, if
buyer goes quiet or short, mirroring a last word can restart them. Labeling is great when an
objection has an emotional component (“It sounds like you’ve had a bad past experience that’s
making you cautious.”).
• Contraindications: Don’t label someone’s emotion incorrectly or patronizingly. E.g., don’t say “You
seem angry” if they’re just mildly annoyed – could irritate them more. Better to hedge: “It sounds like
you might be frustrated about X.” They can correct if wrong. Also avoid labeling too frequently or
obviously reading off a script. It should feel natural. Mirroring: don’t over-mirror every sentence, it

• Risk Profile: Low if done sincerely. Chris Voss notes people love to be labeled correctly, it calms them
17 . If wrong, they’ll correct you – which is still engagement. The risk is minor: they might think “Yes,

• Labeling:

Maneuver: Commitment / Micro-Yes Sequencing
• Intent: Gain a series of small affirmative commitments (micro-yeses) that psychologically lead the
buyer towards a final yes. This tactic is about breaking the sales process into tiny agreements – each
one is easy to say yes to – thereby increasing the likelihood of agreement at the end due to
consistency bias (people like to stay consistent with prior yes). It also maintains engagement by
getting the buyer to actively respond positively throughout. This addresses scenarios where a big
ask upfront might scare them, so you instead ask incremental questions like “Does this make sense
so far?”, “Would solving this be a priority for you if possible?” – The buyer’s “yes” to these sets the
stage for the ultimate yes.
• Best Triggers: Throughout the call as you transition phases. For example, after explaining a concept,
ask “Am I understanding your situation correctly?” or “Does that sound accurate to you?” (easy yes if
you were right). When scheduling next steps: “Would it make sense to involve your partner at that
stage?” If they say yes, they’re mentally committing. In closing, rather than “Will you buy?” you might
ask a series: “Does this solution address your needs? (yes) Do you feel comfortable with the
implementation plan? (yes) So is there anything else stopping us from moving forward? (if no, then
close).” It’s also a way to test if you’ve missed something.
• Contraindications: Don’t overdo with obvious or patronizing yes-questions (“This is great, right? …
You want to improve profits, right?” Too cheesy). Also, avoid leading yes questions on contentious
points; if you suspect a no, better to surface the no (e.g., “Would this timeline be an issue for you?” –
they might say no, which is fine but not a micro-yes). Micro-yeses should be for things likely to be
affirmative to maintain positive momentum. Also, be genuine: each should serve a purpose (confirm
understanding, confirm value, etc.), not just a string of “yes man” questions.
• Risk Profile: Low, if used naturally. The main risk is sounding like a slick salesperson if you do it too
transparently. If buyer notices “They’re just trying to get me to say yes a bunch,” it can backfire. The
model should space them and tie them to actual content.
• Required Authority Level: None specific; any rep can ask for confirmations. But the trust should be
there enough that buyer doesn’t feel manipulated by being agreeable.

• Execution Sequence:
• Identify key points in conversation to confirm alignment (problem definition, needs, value of

• Formulate a question that expects a yes if things are on track. Often start with “Would you agree

• Ask and get the yes (or if no, then address why).

Great, let’s explore some.”

Maneuver: Objection Handling – Absorb vs. Deflect
• Intent: Two broad strategies to handle objections: Absorption means fully acknowledging and
addressing the objection (even if that means admitting a shortcoming and then providing
reassurance or solution), while Deflection means temporarily setting aside the objection to not
derail the conversation (promising to address later or reframing away from it). Elite sellers choose
the approach based on whether the objection is real and critical or a smokescreen/minor issue.
32

• Best Triggers (Absorb): When buyer raises a serious, deal-breaking concern or something
emotional (e.g., “I’m worried your company is too new,” or “This feature is a must-have and you don’t
have it.”). If it’s a valid concern, absorb it: empathize and address it thoroughly. Also when an
objection keeps recurring – you must absorb because deflecting won’t work forever.
• Best Triggers (Deflect): Early objections that are premature or off-topic (“Just send me a proposal”
early on, or “What about feature X?” when you know it’s not crucial). Also price questions early, often
deflect to value/process as we discussed. If the objection seems like a reflex or brush-off (like “I’m
not interested” at the start), deflect by e.g. ignoring once or twisting into a question (“Sure, many
people aren’t at first – can I ask one thing...”). Use deflection when addressing it now would sidetrack
the call or when you suspect it’s not the real issue.
• Contraindications: Don’t deflect a genuine, heartfelt objection too many times – buyer will feel
ignored. Conversely, don’t over-elaborate (absorb) a tiny issue and waste time. Also, when deflecting,
do so gracefully; never say “we’ll deal with that later” in a dismissive tone. Instead promise a proper
address at a specific point (“Great question, let’s park that – I have a slide on that in a minute.”).
• Risk Profile: Medium. Handling objections is critical: failing to do so can lose deals. But picking
wrong strategy has risk: deflecting when they needed an answer can frustrate; absorbing too deeply
can open more questions or emphasize a weakness. This is where judgment is key. Our system will
evaluate the objection type (common trivial vs major category) and buyer’s tone to recommend.
• Required Authority Level: Some trust needed for deflection (“trust me we’ll get to it”). If trust is low,
maybe give a quick partial answer before deferring. For absorption, no special requirement but
handling well can increase your credibility.
• Execution Sequence (Absorb):
• Hear them out fully: Don’t cut off. Maybe mirror or label to show understanding: “It sounds like
uptime guarantee is a big worry for you.”
• Empathize/Validate: “I totally understand – if I were in your shoes, I’d be concerned about that
too.” (This lowers defenses).
• Address directly with facts or plans: “Our current uptime is 99.8%. We also include penalties in
contract if we go below 99.5%. And we can offer customer references who had the same worry but
are now confident in our reliability.” Provide whatever info to satisfy the concern.
• Check if resolved: “Does that help address your concern about uptime, or is there something else
you’d like to see regarding that?” If they say it’s resolved, great. If not, ask what would make them
comfortable – maybe it’s a trial or SLA clause.
• Only then move on.
• Execution Sequence (Deflect):
• Acknowledge briefly (if needed): “I hear your question about X.”
• Give a reason to postpone: “To give you the best answer, can we circle back to that after I show you
Y? It will make more sense with context.” Or “That’s actually coming up in a later part of our

discussion; if you don’t mind, I’ll cover it then.” Or if at beginning: “Absolutely, I can send you a
proposal – maybe I can just gather a few details now so it’s accurate?”
• Bridge back to agenda: Have a plan to ensure you do address it later. Maybe write it down visibly if
in person (“noting your question on the board to hit later”).
• Follow through: When the planned time comes, bring it up: “Earlier you asked about X, here’s the
answer...”

• Absorb:

Maneuver: De-escalation and Apology (Service Recovery)
(A specific maneuver in case of an angry or upset customer)
• Intent: When a prospect or customer is angry (maybe due to a service failure or something that
went wrong), the goal is to calm them down, show empathy, take ownership, and resolve or
plan to resolve the issue. This is less about selling and more about saving the relationship so that
a sale can continue or a churn is prevented. It’s a bit of customer service injected into sales.
• Best Triggers: Customer/prospect expresses anger or high frustration, possibly with strong
language or clear upset tone. E.g., “Your tech was supposed to be here at 10 and it’s noon!” or “This
is the third time I’m explaining this, I’m really annoyed.” Also if they threaten to cancel or not buy
because of an issue. This is critical to handle immediately.
• Contraindications: None really – if someone is upset, de-escalation is almost always the first step.
Unless the person is abusive, then professional boundaries (but that is outside selling scope, maybe
escalate to manager if abusive). But in normal upset scenario, de-escalate always before trying to
rationalize or sell more.
• Risk Profile: If done well, can completely turn situation around (some of the best loyalty is built by
recovering well from mistakes). If done poorly (defensive or dismissive), you definitely lose the deal/
customer. High stakes. But straightforward method: apologize sincerely, etc.
• Required Authority Level: Not authority in expertise, but authority to solve their problem. If you
can say “I will personally ensure this is fixed,” that’s strong. If you’re a sales rep who needs help from

• Execution Sequence:
• Listen fully: Let them vent without interruption until they pause.
• Apologize and acknowledge: Even if it’s not your fault, apologize on behalf of company. “I’m really
sorry this happened. This is not the experience we want you to have.” Use a calm, genuine tone.
Acknowledge specifics: “Waiting since 10 and still no tech – I would be frustrated too.”
• Empathize: “I completely understand why you’re upset.” Sometimes label: “It seems you feel like
we’ve let you down – and frankly, we have in this instance.”
• Take ownership: “I’m going to take ownership of this and get it resolved for you.” People want to
hear that someone is accountable and will fix it.

Maneuver: Strategic Patience (Inaction as Action)
• Intent: Knowing when not to take any major action and let things play out or gather more info.
This is more of a meta-strategy – sometimes the best move is to do nothing immediately rather than
react impulsively. In sales, this could mean not jumping to offer a discount at the first hint of price
concern, or not overloading a silent prospect with follow-ups (just one gentle nudge and then give
space). It’s about having the patience to allow the buyer to process or to let a developing situation
clarify itself.
• Best Triggers: When a prospect is unresponsive but not officially lost (maybe they said “we need two
weeks”), the strategy might be to not bombard them, but wait or gently check in only once with
value. Or in a negotiation when you gave a proposal and they haven’t responded in the usual time –
sometimes waiting an extra day rather than chasing can prompt them to come back (they might be
checking internally). Also on a call: if buyer is thinking deeply (you can see they’re pondering), staying
quiet or not adding new info is strategic patience.
In coaching context, if rep did an action and wants to see outcome, sometimes waiting is prudent rather
than immediately switching strategy.
• Contraindications: Don’t confuse inaction with neglect. If a buyer explicitly asks for info or has a
concern, you do act. Strategic patience is purposeful – you expect that acting too soon might harm.
Also, not to be used as excuse for laziness: it should be a conscious choice (like “I won’t follow up for
3 days because they said they’re on vacation until then”).
• Risk Profile: Low. Often doing nothing immediate has minimal risk, except the internal anxiety. But
if misapplied, could let a deal go cold. That’s why it’s strategic – maybe you set a calendar reminder
to act later. The risk is mainly missing an opportunity if you wait too long. The model can mitigate by
setting a “review in X days” plan.
• Required Authority Level: Not relevant.
• Execution Example:
• After sending a proposal, buyer said they’ll review by next week. The rep might be eager to ping in 2
days. The AI might say: “No action needed today. Give them until the agreed time. We’ll follow up the
day after their stated review date if no word. In the meantime, perhaps prepare additional ROI data
so you’re ready.”
• Or a prospect said “call me in a month.” Strategic patience: mark it and actually wait a month (maybe
send one email in between with a relevant insight, not a sales push).
• On a live call, if buyer is quietly calculating something, strategic patience is basically the silence
maneuver – let them do it.
• Another: If the buyer’s company is going through something (merger, crisis), sometimes best to
pause sales efforts and check back when dust settles, rather than push now.
• What Can Go Wrong: If you misread and wait but the competitor swoops in. That’s why it’s
important to confirm if waiting is okay (“You mentioned to reconnect in a month – I’ll do that.” so
37

they assent). If there’s no explicit timeline, an alternative approach is to ask: “When should I follow
up?” They say, “Give me two weeks.” Then strategic patience is to indeed wait two weeks, not 2 days.
This maneuver acknowledges that sometimes inaction is a deliberate strategy – a sophisticated concept often
lacking in rigid playbooks. GoMotto will incorporate patience by not always giving an immediate task if
context suggests that breathing room is needed (like not advising to send a proposal the same day if it
might come across desperate – maybe suggest sending next morning, etc., small stuff but adds up to
polish).
Each of these maneuvers has a place in the conversation. There is never one universally correct
maneuver – context is king. Top sales pros often have to choose among a few paths. Our playbook’s job is
to equip GoMotto (and the reps using it) with a rich set of options and the wisdom of when each is likely to
succeed or backfire, as detailed above.

4) Tactical Language System: “Soft Rules,” Not Hard Rules
Language matters immensely in sales, but context is everything. There are few absolute “always say this,
never say that” rules – instead, we develop soft rules and guidelines that adapt to the persona and
situation. GoMotto’s language engine must calibrate wording to maintain authority, build trust, and
resonate with the buyer’s background, all while avoiding common sales language pitfalls.
Key elements of the language system:
• Persona-Based Lexicon: The AI should modulate vocabulary and style based on who the buyer is
(e.g., a blue-collar homeowner vs. a corporate procurement officer vs. a tech-savvy engineer). For
example:
• A homeowner might prefer simple, relatable terms. If selling a solar panel to a homeowner, say
“monthly electric bill” instead of “kilowatt-hour consumption.” Use analogies (“as sturdy as a roof can
be”) and avoid corporate buzzwords. A bit of warmth and casual tone is okay (“folks,” “your family’s
comfort”).
• A procurement officer or technical buyer expects precise language, maybe some industry jargon to
show credibility. For them, avoid overly emotional appeals; focus on metrics, ROI, specs. Use terms
they use (“capex,” “ROI,” “SLAs”). Professional tone, maybe more formal address (“you mentioned
earlier, Ms. Lee, that…”) whereas a homeowner might be first names.
• A small business owner might like a mix: professional but not too stiff, appreciate directness and
practical language (“let’s get more customers through your door” rather than abstract “optimize
revenue streams”).
38

The system might have a persona mapping: e.g., [Homeowner persona: use everyday language, avoid
buzzwords, don’t assume technical knowledge, reference family/home]. [Executive persona: use confident,
concise language, hit financial metrics, avoid slang].
• Contextual Word Choices (“Cheap” vs “Cost-Effective” etc.): Certain words can be good or bad
depending on context:
• “Cheap”: If a buyer themselves says “I need something cheap,” mirroring their word carefully could
be okay, but generally cheap can imply low quality. Prefer “affordable” or “cost-effective” when
talking about your solution’s cost advantage, as it emphasizes value for cost 59 . Example: Instead of
“our product is cheaper,” say “our solution is more cost-effective while delivering the same results.”
However, if you sense the buyer equates low cost with smart buying, you might say “lower cost” or
“budget-friendly.” Use “cheap” only if quoting them or differentiating (e.g., “I wouldn’t want to give
you something cheap that fails in a year; I’d rather focus on quality within your budget.”).
• Specificity vs. Softness: When to be blunt vs diplomatic. For instance, technical buyers appreciate
specificity: “a 17% failure rate” is better than “sometimes fails.” For a delicate consumer concern,
softness helps: “a bit of wear and tear” vs “major damage” if you’re easing them in. Rule of thumb: Be
specific when giving positive info or neutral facts (build credibility). Be diplomatic when
discussing potentially negative or sensitive points (to not alarm or offend). Also, when closing
or giving a direct ask, often a straightforward approach works (“Can we move forward with the
agreement?”) rather than overly hedging (“Would you maybe like to proceed if it’s okay with you?” -
too wobbly).
• Positive Framing: Use positive phrasing instead of negative where possible. Example: Instead of
“We can’t meet until next week,” say “We can certainly meet next week; I have Tuesday open.” It’s the
same info but avoids a negative impression.
• Avoiding “Salesy” clichés: Phrases that instantly reduce trust include: “Honestly,...” or “Trust me,” (if
you have to say trust me, you sound untrustworthy 60 ), overused rapport lines (“weather talk” that
feels forced), overly enthusiastic promises (“This will change your life!” – unless context warrants
excitement). Instead of “I guarantee this will solve all your problems,” say “I’m confident based on
[evidence] this can solve [specific problem].” Remove filler apologetic words: “Just checking in,”
“hopefully,” “I think maybe…” – these weaken authority. For example, “I just wanted to check if maybe
you had time” vs “I’m following up to see if you had time to review my proposal.” The latter is
assertive but polite.
• Pressure Language vs. Invitation Language: Avoid language that pushes (“We need to close this
now because quota/pressure reasons”, or “You should really...”). Instead use inviting language
(“What would it take on your end to move forward?” or “Shall we aim to get this done by Friday to
keep the momentum?” – still a nudge but cooperative). Use “we” to signal partnership in solving
something, rather than repeatedly saying “I” or “you must”.

• Language Anti-Patterns List: What reduces authority or trust:
• Overuse of Filler/Hedges: “maybe, kind of, I think, hopefully, just, like” – these make the rep sound
unsure. The model should flag excessive hedge words. Of course, be polite (“I think” can soften a
suggestion), but often it’s stronger to drop them.
41

• Up-speak or Tentative Tone: If transcribing tone was possible, it’d note if statements sound like
questions (common in unsure reps). While our system can’t fully control a human’s tone, it can
encourage confident wording that leads to confident tone.
• Slang or Jargon Misuse: Using slang with a formal buyer (“this is gonna be awesome, dude”) – no.
Or heavy jargon to a layperson. The AI should choose jargon only if buyer uses it or persona fits. A
home services rep shouldn’t rattle off technical part numbers to a homeowner who just cares if it
works.
• Negative talk about others: E.g., bashing competitors (“Their product is garbage”) – looks
unprofessional and can erode trust. Better to say “I can’t speak for them; what we focus on is X.” So
avoid direct slander or too-negative comparisons. Also avoid complaining about other clients or
internal things – always keep it positive and client-focused.
• Self-focused statements: Too many “I” or bragging (“I’m the best at this, I crushed my quota”).
Focus on “you” (the buyer) and “we” (partnership). If needing to highlight achievement, frame it as
“We’ve been fortunate to help 100 clients...” rather than “I did 100 deals.”
• Overpromising absolute words: “Always, never, guaranteed to double your revenue.” These set
unrealistic expectations or sound like a typical sales hype. Use measured optimism: “Our clients
typically see...”
• Stacking questions / interrogative tone: Asking multiple questions rapid-fire without giving them
space – can feel like an interrogation. Also a language pattern to avoid.
• Insincere flattery: e.g., telling a prospect “You’re the best client I’ve talked to” when obviously not
genuine – buyers have a nose for flattery. Keep compliments specific and real if any (“You asked
some really insightful questions about ROI, which I appreciate” – genuine).
• Defensive language: like “Actually, that’s not what I said...” or “No, you’re wrong,” – even if buyer
misunderstood, rephrase positively: “I think I might not have explained clearly...” rather than “you
misunderstood.”
In implementation, GoMotto will have a linguistic style module. It could dynamically adjust synonyms to fit
the tone (like the earlier examples: “authorize” vs “sign”). We’ll also integrate these guidelines into prompt
libraries – e.g., if a rep types an email or the AI drafts a follow-up, it can flag “just checking in” and suggest
the improved variant.
Importantly, the system’s not imposing a single script but rather enforcing quality control on language. For
instance, if a rep says something potentially bad like “Honestly, the competitor’s product is awful,” the AI
could suggest a rephrase or warn in real-time (“Rephrase to avoid negative talk about competitor; focus on
our value.”).
Now we address how major sales frameworks feed into our strategy without being followed religiously:
Frameworks as Inputs, Not Religions
The sales world has many frameworks – NEPQ, SPIN, Challenger, Sandler, MEDDIC, Consultative Selling, etc.
Each offers useful concepts, but none is a silver bullet applicable verbatim to every situation. Our
philosophy is to decompose these frameworks into primitives and maneuvers that the AI can flexibly
use.
42

For each framework, consider:
• NEPQ (Neuro-Emotional Persuasion Questioning): Developed by Jeremy Miner, it emphasizes
asking layered questions to surface emotion and pain, and uses a lot of diffusing language (“I’m not
sure if we could even help – [pullback] – but if I could ask...”). It aligns with our maneuvers like
Pullback, Curiosity Loop. Contribution: A structured way to go from problem awareness to
implication to solution by questions. It heavily uses labeling feelings and understanding the
emotional drivers. Limitations: It can be slow-paced; in some transactional sales, you can’t ask 30
questions. Also, not every buyer will have the patience if done formulaically. AI use: Incorporate
NEPQ-style questions in the Curiosity Loop maneuver, use the pullback/disqualify exactly as NEPQ
teaches (we outlined that sequence). Also phrases like “What were you hoping to see from...?” to
gauge ideal outcome – a NEPQ tactic. But we won’t have AI just recite a NEPQ script – instead it picks
the right question at right time.
• SPIN Selling: Neil Rackham’s research-based method focusing on Situation, Problem, Implication,
Need-Payoff questions 52 . Contribution: The importance of implication questions (we
implemented in Curiosity Loop – asking consequences of problem) 29 and need-payoff (getting
them to state the benefit they seek). It’s a foundational questioning technique and reminds us to not
pitch too early – let questions build the need. Limitations: It doesn’t explicitly cover handling
objections or closing strongly – it assumes if you do SPIN well, close is natural (often true, but not
always). Also, SPIN was for large sales where time is ample; in short sales, you compress that. AI
use: The questioning structure heavily influences our approach to discovery. The AI can tag
questions as P, I, or N type and ensure we cover them. We’ll use SPIN as a guideline for content of
questions. We won’t stick to the acronym in interface, but under the hood, conversation design is
informed by it.
• The Challenger Sale: Distinct in that it advocates teaching with insights, tailoring, and taking control
of the sale 11 . It identified that “relationship builders” who just accommodate are less effective in
complex sales 3 . Contribution: Emphasis on reframing customer thinking (we have maneuvers for
reframing to risk or insight), on assertiveness and maintaining constructive tension 68 . Also on
personalization of value messages (tailoring). Limitations: Suits complex B2B high change-inducing
sales. For simpler or very relationship-driven contexts, a pure Challenger approach can seem too
pushy. Also it requires reps to have insight to offer – not trivial. If AI can ingest industry data, it can
generate insight (“Did you know 53% of loyalty is due to sales experience? 19 etc.), effectively
enabling a Challenger approach at scale. AI use: We incorporate challenger by training AI to provide
industry or process insights as part of recommendations (“This is a chance to challenge the status
quo: perhaps share a statistic about X”). The Authority Anchor and Reframe maneuvers embody
Challenger elements. And we caution AI not to default to relationship-building only; sometimes a
respectful challenge or pushback (like the disqualification move) is the better call, echoing
Challenger philosophy.
• Sandler Selling System: Notable for concepts like the upfront contract (setting agenda and mutual
agreement for a call), pain funnel (drilling down pain), and the “negative reverse” (similar to our
pullback: saying the opposite of the expected). Contribution: The idea of always having clear next
steps (upfront contract) and mutual agreement – e.g., at call start, Sandler suggests saying “Is it
okay if I ask you some questions and then you can ask me? And at the end, if it seems like a fit, we
discuss next steps; if not, we part as friends?” That sets a low-pressure frame. Also the negative
43

reversing: “Maybe this isn’t a fit” to get them to argue it is – we have that. Limitations: It’s a bit old-
school and can be too formulaic. But its core is sound on setting expectations. AI use: The AI can
incorporate something like the upfront contract at beginning of calls or meetings – it could prompt a
rep: “Establish agenda and permission: e.g., ‘We have 30 minutes, my goal is to understand X and
then show Y, is that okay?’” That improves call structure. We already use negative reverse in pullback.
Sandler’s emphasis on uncovering pain and budget – we cover pain via questions, budget we cover
but carefully (not always ask up front, except maybe in qualification stage for transactional deals –
the AI will adapt by deal type).
• MEDDIC (for qualification in complex B2B): Metrics, Economic Buyer, Decision Criteria, Decision
Process, Identify Pain, Champion. Contribution: It’s not a selling conversation framework but a deal
qualification checklist to ensure you have all the info. For AI, it’s relevant in signal extraction
(section 5) – e.g., detect if economic buyer is mentioned or present; if not, coach rep to find out.
Identify pain – we do. Champion – maybe out of scope for conversational AI to identify, but could
hint if someone seems supportive internally. Limitations: Strictly for internal deal review and to
ensure forecasting accuracy. AI use: We won’t bore a homeowner with MEDDIC questions, but in
B2B context, the AI can prompt things like “We haven’t discussed decision process; consider asking
who else is involved or timeline.” That’s MEDDIC concept. Or ensure pain is clearly identified by end
of discovery.
• Consultative Selling (solution selling, etc.): General approach to be a consultant diagnosing and
solving problems rather than a product pusher. Contribution: The overall ethos of our whole system
aligns here: be buyer-centric, ask, listen, tailor a solution. It’s where frameworks like SPIN and
Challenger overlap anyway. Limitation: Sometimes consultative selling is interpreted as never ask for
the sale, just help – which can lead to wimpy closing. Our approach is consultative but with goal
orientation and closing acumen.
How we decompose frameworks into AI-usable components:
We basically have done it in our maneuvers and signals: - From NEPQ: pullback, validating questions,
emotional labeling. - From SPIN: problem/implication Qs sequence. - From Challenger: reframes, insight
drops, and assertiveness. - From Sandler: upfront contract, negative reverse (disqualify), not needing the
sale more than the prospect does (which we do via authority and disqualify stance). - From MEDDIC: ensure
signals for decision process and metrics (ROI). - From Consultative: everything about focusing on buyer
needs and being an advisor (which sums up our entire design).
When following frameworks blindly fails: - If one tried to just mechanically do SPIN, they might sound like a
questionnaire and not adapt. Our AI will mix the questions with active discussion. - A pure Challenger who
pushes insight without listening could bulldoze the buyer – fail. Our AI will balance empathy and challenge
(based on trust level). - NEPQ’s soft tone everywhere might make a rep sound too tentative if the buyer
expects confidence. So we pick moments for soft tonality (like in pullback or initial questions) but when it’s
time to state price or solution, we go confident. - Sandler’s upfront contract if delivered awkwardly can
confuse a prospect (“why are you talking about ending as friends?”). So AI can suggest a modernized form:
set agenda and outcome expectation in a friendly way.
In conclusion, we treat these frameworks as toolkits. GoMotto’s intelligence is the master craftsman
selecting the right tool for the job in real time, rather than following one fixed toolkit blueprint for every job.

At this point, we have defined behaviors, strategies, tactics, and language frameworks. Next, we need the
system to perceive what’s happening in calls – that’s Context & Signal Extraction – and then how to choose
strategies among many, evaluate reps, etc. Let’s proceed to how the AI detects the conversation signals
needed to activate these maneuvers and decisions.
5) Context & Signal Extraction: What to Detect From Calls
For GoMotto to coach effectively, it must first observe and interpret what’s happening in the conversation.
This means extracting a rich set of signals from call data (transcripts, audio cues, etc.) in real-time or post-
call. We define a taxonomy of signals the system should detect, and for each, how it influences maneuver
selection or evaluation.
Key Signal Categories to Extract:
1. Buyer Intent & Urgency: Are they just browsing or actively needing a solution soon? Indicators:
2. Language: “We’re just gathering info” vs. “We need this solved by next month.”
3. Questions: If buyer asks many specific questions about implementation or price, likely serious.
4. Tone: An eager or urgent tone vs. a casual, non-committal tone.
5. Context: If they inbound called from an urgent need (e.g., AC broke in summer), urgency high.
6. Use: If urgency high, the AI might accelerate certain moves (closing steps, scheduling soon,
emphasizing speed of delivery). If intent seems low, perhaps focus on education and nurture (less
pressure, more building need first).
7. Buyer’s Emotional State & Volatility: Are they calm, excited, frustrated, skeptical?
8. Tone analysis: Elevated pitch or volume -> possibly excitement or anger; slow, flat tone -> maybe
disinterest or sad.
9. Words indicating feeling: “I’m concerned,” “This is frustrating,” “love this,” “hate that.”
10. Pace and interruption: If buyer starts cutting off or speaking fast, could be agitation or eagerness.
11. Use: If emotion is high (positive or negative), that cues different strategies. Excitement from buyer
means maybe move to close or reinforce. Anger triggers de-escalation (as discussed). Skepticism
triggers trust maneuvers (social proof, data). A depressed or low-energy prospect might need more
enthusiasm or prompting from our side.
12. Trust/Skepticism Signals: Does the buyer trust the rep? Are they doubtful of claims?
13. Speech content: Expressions like “I’m not sure I believe that,” “That sounds too good to be true,”
clearly show skepticism.
14. Body language (if video): arms crossed, frowning. (Not sure if AI has that input, maybe later
integration.)
15. Level of disclosure: A sign of trust is the buyer volunteering information (pain, budget). If they hold
cards close and only give minimal info, trust might be low.
16. Response to social proof: If you mention another client, do they lean in (“oh who else uses this?”) or
remain cold (“that doesn’t matter to me”).
45

17. Use: Low trust -> need Authority Anchor or more credibility building, perhaps slower ask. High trust -
> you can be more direct, even challenge them since they trust your intent. The AI might score trust
in real-time (like sentiment + engagement) and use it to adapt tone (more formal vs. friendly) and
strategy (e.g., if trust is low, avoid heavy closing, focus on building credibility first).
18. Buyer Engagement Level: Are they actively participating or just giving yes/no minimal answers?
19. Turn-taking ratio: If the buyer talks a decent percentage (40-60%), engagement is good 1 . If rep is
doing 90% of talking, buyer likely disengaged.
20. Types of responses: Engaged buyer asks questions, gives full answers. Disengaged gives one-word
answers or delays (“uh-huh, sure”).
21. Presence of distractions: If background noise of them typing or they keep saying “what was that?”
they might not be paying full attention.
22. Use: If engagement is dropping, AI might suggest re-engagement maneuvers (ask an open
question, call their name, or check in: “I want to make sure I’m addressing what you care about – is
this resonating?”). If highly engaged, keep momentum, maybe introduce more advanced topics or
go for micro-commitments.
23. Financial Sensitivity (Budget Signals): Is cost a major concern for them?
24. Explicit statements: “We have a tight budget,” “It’s too expensive,” “Money is an issue.”
25. Price questions early: If their first question is cost, they are likely very price-sensitive or budget-
limited.
26. The persona: e.g., a public sector procurement tends to be very cost-driven. A wealthy homeowner
might be more quality-driven. But signals matter more than stereotypes.
27. Reaction to quote: Did they flinch or say “okay”?
28. Use: If high budget sensitivity, maneuvers: Price Anchoring carefully, more emphasis on ROI (so they
see value>cost), maybe introduce smaller options (Scope control). If cost is not a big issue (some say
“budget is no problem, I want the best”), focus on premium value and don’t push discounts or
cheapen it.
29. Decision Structure: Who is involved in the decision? Is the person on the call the decision-maker, an
influencer, or a gatekeeper? Multi-person committees?
30. Phrases: “I need to run this by my [boss/partner/board].” – indicates others involved.
31. Titles/roles: If your contact is mid-level, likely they need higher approval. If spouse not present and
they mention them, that's a joint decision sign.
32. Number of people in meetings: If multiple people join from their side, possibly a committee or group
decision.
33. MEDDIC indicators: economic buyer identified or not, champion present (hard to auto-detect
champion, but if one person is very positive and pushing, they might be).
34. Use: If key decision maker is not present, the AI should prompt the rep to either arrange a meeting
with them or equip the current contact with info for them. Also, adapt close strategy: you might go
for a softer close (like next meeting with boss) rather than pressing this person to sign. Identify if
you have a champion (someone who likes our solution and can internally sell it) – if yes, treat them
46

well, give them ammo. If you only have a coach (not decision maker and not champion), strategy is
to find a champion or DM by requesting involvement.
35. Hidden Objections vs. Stated Objections: Sometimes the reason they give isn’t the real reason. AI
can detect discrepancy or uncertainty:
36. Voice hesitancy: e.g., they say “I need to think about it” but their tone is uncertain – likely a hidden
objection (maybe price or lack of trust).
37. Patterns: If they keep delaying or raising minor issues, underlying cause might be bigger (like they
don’t see value, or a stakeholder not convinced).
38. If they've not asked any questions about a certain critical area (like they didn't ask features at all but
stall to “think,” perhaps the real issue is something else).
39. Use: If AI suspects a hidden objection (like consistently deferring decision without saying why), it
might suggest maneuvers to flush it out, e.g., “pullback” (which often surfaces the truth as they
protest). Or a direct approach: “Often when people say they need to think about it, there’s usually a
particular concern at the back of their mind – can I ask if that’s the case for you?” This labeling of the
situation can encourage them to reveal it. Also the AI might mark calls with unresolved objections for
post-call coaching: rep needs to learn to surface those.
40. Stage of the Buyer’s Journey (Decision Stage Signals): Are they early research, mid evaluation, or
ready to buy?
41. Language about alternatives: “We are looking at a few options” (evaluation stage), vs. “I just started
exploring” (early), vs. “We need a final proposal by Friday” (late stage, near decision).
42. Questions type: Early stage asks broad “what does it do?”; mid stage asks comparison or detailed
“how do you handle X?”; late stage might be legal/contractual questions or implementation
scheduling.
43. Commitment signals: If buyer talks about “when we implement” or asks about onboarding, that’s a
buying signal – they’re already envisioning owning it (late stage signal).
44. Use: Stage helps in strategy: Early stage – focus on building need and differentiating (maybe more
Challenger insights to provoke thought). Mid stage – ensure all concerns addressed, provide proof,
perhaps trial. Late stage – focus on closing, simplifying paperwork, reinforcing decision confidence
(no new big info that could derail). The AI should identify cues and adjust recommendations
accordingly (e.g., don’t push for close in first discovery call if clearly early stage; instead aim for next
step).
45. Power Dynamics / Status Tests: As discussed, signals of power shift: e.g., buyer interrupts
frequently or challenges rep’s knowledge (“Are you sure about that stat?” in a testing tone). Or buyer
behavior like being late or multitasking could be a subtle status move. Also, buyer asking pointed
questions to test rep’s competence.
46. Use: Recognize when rep might be losing alpha position. If buyer is testing, AI could suggest a
confident reassertion (like provide a fact, or ask a thought-provoking question back). If buyer tries to
dominate schedule (“I only have 5 min now, do your pitch”), rep could salvage with a mini upfront
contract (“I can do 5 min high-level and if you like we schedule more”). The key is not to grovel. The
47

system might flag when rep uses too many weak phrases (sign of submissiveness) or if buyer keeps
dictating conversation (maybe rep needs to step up with more direct questions to steer).
47. Compliance / SOP Adherence Points: From the company’s perspective, certain things must be said
or asked (like disclaimers, or following an SOP for qualifying). E.g., a solar company might require
rep to ask about electric bill, roof condition, and to mention the federal tax credit (a compliance
thing for proper quoting). The AI should detect if those points were covered.
◦ If not, prompt rep (post-call or even live “Don’t forget to ask if they have permission from
HOA,” etc.).
◦ Also any regulatory compliance – e.g. in finance, have to mention APR clearly, etc.
◦ Use: Ensure thoroughness and avoid liability. Also in post-call, if rep deviated but succeeded,
note it as deliberate possibly (some top reps deviate from script if beneficial, which might be
fine unless it’s compliance-critical).
Each signal maps to maneuvers and coaching: - For example, buyer says “I don’t trust your company’s
experience.” Signals: trust issue, skepticism. Maneuvers: Authority Anchor (share experience), Social proof
(share other client results), Label (“It sounds like our relative newness is a concern”), perhaps Reframe to
Process (“Would a trial help alleviate that concern? Let’s do a pilot.”). - Another: buyer consistently returns
to price (“But it’s so much money.”) Signals: budget sensitivity. Maneuvers: Price anchoring (remind them
of problem cost), Scope adjust, Payment plan maybe (if applicable), or even Pullback if they’re fixated (“It
might be that we’re not the right solution if price is the main factor, and that’s okay.”) to test if it’s a
smokescreen or real. - If buyer is quiet and giving yes/no: Signal low engagement. Maneuver: Curiosity
questions (ask about their situation more), maybe label the silence (“I get the feeling this might not be a top
priority right now, is that fair?” which could either get them to open up or allow disqualification). - Buyer
mentions competitor X in positive light: Signal: they are comparing, maybe leaning competitor.
Maneuvers: Enemy framing (if appropriate, not directly competitor but perhaps highlight where competitor

because [value].”), and definitely differentiate on criteria they care about. - Buyer gives “call me later” or
“send me info” early: Signal: likely brush-off. Maneuver: Deflect (as we did: “Sure, can do – before I do,
mind if I ask...”), or Pullback (“I sense maybe this isn’t a priority for you right now – and that’s okay.” Silence).
- Buyer is angry about service (if this was an existing customer call): Signal: conflict. Maneuver: De-
escalation (apology, etc.), possibly involve support team.
To implement, the AI will use: - NLP on transcripts for key words/phrases. - Sentiment analysis for tone
(some conversation AI do this: positive, neutral, negative sentiment scoring). - Voice analysis (if accessible)
for pitch and pace as proxies for excitement or anger. - Conversation context: e.g., if rep asked about

We will design a labeling scheme where each moment in call could be annotated (for machine learning) as,
say, [OBJECTION: Price], [QUESTION: Implementation], [EMOTION: Skepticism], [DECISION_MAKER: “my

(Challenger approach to wake them up). - If time is short (explicit or you sense rush) -> Maneuver:

This closes the loop of perceiving context. Now with those signals, how do we choose among multiple valid
strategies?
6) Choosing Among Multiple Valid Strategies
Often, there isn’t a single “right” move – maybe two or three different maneuvers could work. The AI needs
a framework to evaluate and choose the best-fit strategy for the context, and also adapt if the initial
strategy doesn’t land well.
We propose a decision framework / rubric for selecting strategies, considering factors like: effectiveness
(likelihood to solve the issue), risk (if it fails, how bad?), alignment with buyer’s personality, and consistency
with long-term relationship.
Strategy Selection Rubric (Scoring Criteria):
1. Context Fit Score: Does the strategy directly address the buyer’s main current concern or stage?
(e.g., If buyer’s objection is about trust, a trust-building strategy scores high; a price strategy might
score low). We check: does it align with signals? Score 1-5.
2. Pressure/Impact Score: How will this strategy affect the pressure and frame? If buyer is already
defensive, a strategy that eases pressure (like pullback) might score higher than one that increases it
(like a hard close). If buyer is disengaged, maybe a slightly higher pressure (like challenging
question) is needed. Rate which approach would likely improve engagement without breaking trust.
3. Risk Score: What’s the downside if this strategy is wrong? E.g., using humor or a bold challenge with
a very conservative buyer could offend (high risk). A gentle question has low risk but maybe also low
immediate reward. The AI can assign a penalty for risk.
4. Relationship Stage / Authority: Is the rep’s relationship strong enough for this strategy? If not, a
strategy requiring high trust (like an outright ask for sale or a strong challenge) might be riskier. If
yes, more aggressive move might be okay. So “prerequisite trust” check: if trust < threshold, down-
score strategies that rely on high trust.
5. Success Likelihood from data: Over time, the system can learn from outcomes. E.g., in similar
situations (similar signals), which strategies historically led to positive outcomes (like next meeting,
deal closed)? A statistical model could influence the score. If push vs pullback in scenario X, and push
led to drop-offs but pullback led to re-engagement, then lean pullback.

to develop missing skills too.
The output of rubric: The AI would rank options or pick top. For example: - Situation: Buyer says “I need to

maybe they say “yes not a fit bye.” (B) Direct probe might just get another deflection if they aren’t
comfortable revealing. (C) Process (say “take your time, I’ll call in 2 days”) is low pressure, respectful, but
maybe we lose momentum. (D) small incentive could come off as desperate unless they really just need a

yields the truth or flush them out. Score (A) high on addressing issue, medium risk, require moderate

depending on read. If buyer seemed genuinely interested but cautious, maybe (C) – process step to give
them space. If buyer seemed more like brushing off, (A) to test them.
The AI might actually combine: e.g., do a mini-pullback + schedule follow-up (“I get the feeling maybe this
isn’t top priority – which is okay. Why don’t we do this: I’ll send you some info and call next week, and if it’s
not looking beneficial we can call it off.”). That uses both (A) and (C).
How to adapt if buyer reacts poorly: The system should have a feedback loop even within a call. If a

Research and anecdotal evidence on how top reps juggle strategies: - Top reps often have a Plan A, B, C in
mind. If A fails, they smoothly shift rather than freeze. - They listen for what type of objection it is:
Objection vs Smokescreen: e.g. “I need to check with my boss” – a real procedural objection or a polite

Risk-Aware Ranking Example: Say the buyer is extremely analytical and reserved. Two possible closings: a

their thoughts first (“How do you feel about proceeding?”). So the rubric would give a higher rank to the
approach that aligns with buyer’s style.
Our framework formalizes what great sales coaches do in their mind when advising reps: consider the
context, weigh the moves, pick the one that maximizes upside and minimizes downside given the specific
buyer.
The AI can also simulate a quick “mental A/B test”: If I do strategy X, likely outcomes and their probabilities

and preserving relationship if lose).
One more note: if multiple strategies are similarly viable, the AI might pick the one the rep is more

recommend one in real-time so rep isn’t confused. Possibly in training mode, you could show multiple to
teach them. But in live assist, probably single best suggestion.
We also incorporate that sometimes the best approach is a combo maneuver as mentioned – AI can chain
them (e.g., combine a label with a question: label the emotion then ask for next step).

7) How GoMotto Judges If the Rep’s Choice Was Wrong (Post-Call)
One of GoMotto’s deliverables is a post-call coaching report. Here, we need to evaluate the rep’s
performance not just by outcome (sale or no sale) but by the quality of decisions and execution. That means
identifying if the rep did something suboptimal given the context, even if the sale was won (maybe they got
lucky or product sold itself), or if they did the right thing but lost (so we still praise that decision).
We introduce some evaluation metrics (Frame Control, Pressure Management, etc. as given) and how to
compute them:
• Frame Control Score: Did the rep maintain appropriate control and authority in the conversation?
• How to compute: Look for signs of frame loss: e.g., rep frequently interrupts buyer (bad), or buyer
dominates and rep can’t get info (bad), rep not setting agenda.
• Possibly measure: who asked questions more (rep should be asking a lot in discovery – that’s actually
controlling frame by leading direction). If buyer had to ask all the questions, rep was reactive (lower
score).
• Check if rep responded confidently to challenges or if they became defensive or submissive (we can
detect language: too many hedges under pressure, or conceded too quickly).
• It might be partly qualitative: Did the rep guide to a next step or just let it fizzle? If no defined next
step and conversation meandered, frame control was weak.
• Score threshold: e.g., 8/10 means rep led most of call’s structure, answered challenges well. 5/10
means shared control or minor frame breaks. 2/10 means rep was essentially just answering buyer’s
interrogation or lost control.
• Coaching from failure: If frame control low, coach them on setting upfront contract, asking more
questions, not letting tangents go unaddressed.
• Pressure Direction (Pressure Delta) Score: This refers to who was chasing whom by the end, and
did the rep manage pressure properly. Ideally, we want “buyer pressure” (buyer chasing info or next
steps) rather than “seller pressure” (rep pushing).
• Compute: Evaluate key moments: e.g., after pricing, did the buyer ask “what next” (good, buyer
chasing) or did rep keep pushing (“so shall we sign?” with buyer lukewarm).
• Perhaps measure who asked for next step. If rep had to push multiple times (pressure from rep
side), and buyer still hesitant, likely pressure mismanaged.
• Also, check if rep responded to resistance by increasing or decreasing pressure appropriately. For
example, buyer gave objection and rep kept pushing without addressing – that’s bad, pressure
mismanagement.

• A sign of good pressure control: buyer doesn’t feel cornered (maybe they agreed to next step

rep kept pushing), low.

“Sure, it’s important you two decide together. What do you think will be her top questions or
hesitations? Maybe we can go over those now, so you have all the information when you talk.” (This invites
the buyer to voice hidden objections under guise of spouse’s concerns. Bold part tries to surface them. If he says
“Well, she’ll probably ask about financing options,” bingo – you cover that now. If he’s not sure, you can ask
“Would she mainly care about the total cost, or warranty, or...?” to prompt issues. This approach both prepares
him and possibly resolves issues such that spouse might easily agree. If nothing else, it shows you care about
addressing her concerns.) 3. Assure & Pullback slight: “I completely understand – you should discuss with her.
Honestly, I wouldn’t want you to go ahead if she isn’t 100% comfortable either. Why don’t we tentatively pencil in
an installation date a few weeks out, and I’ll hold this price for you. In the meantime, talk with her – and I’m happy
to come by when you’re both home to go over it again.” (This combines reassurance that you’re not pressuring
solo, a small commitment maybe (penciling installation – which actually is a soft close technique but framed
as flexible), and an offer to maintain the deal and assist with spouse. It might create a gentle FOMO by
locking a date, but not in a pushy way – it’s tentative. The bold sentence is a pullback/authority play: “I don’t
want an unhappy spouse/customer,” aligning with their interest. It can build trust that you care about both
of them.) - Signals to Detect: The statement "need to talk to wife" is a clear multi-decisionmaker indicator.
The system flags it. It should then see if rep responds by acknowledging or pushing. If rep immediately says
"Well, she’ll say the same thing I do" or tries an incentive to bypass spouse, that’s bad – AI should intervene
perhaps real-time: “Don’t ignore spouse concern – suggest involving them.” - It also monitors buyer’s
reaction after rep’s response. If rep sets a joint meeting and buyer agrees, great (score context handling
high). If buyer resists scheduling (“No, let me just talk to her first and I’ll get back to you”), might indicate
either they’re still not comfortable or using spouse as excuse. The AI could then advise perhaps a mild
pullback or a question: “Sure, would you feel comfortable if I follow up in a few days after you both chat?” at
least get permission to follow up. If buyer says “I’ll call you,” that’s danger (often means they won’t). The AI
could flag that as a shaky outcome and in follow-up tasks remind rep to proactively reach out after a set
time. - Also the AI should catch if rep doesn’t attempt to schedule or address it at all and just says "Okay,
talk to her and let me know." That’s a missed opportunity. Post-call it would coach: "Better to set a specific
follow-up or offer to speak with both 27 37 . Leaving it open-ended often results in no decision." - Key
68

signals: presence of spouse not in room => multi-decision context. If earlier buyer gave no other objections
but now defers to spouse, could be real or just a delay tactic. The AI might consider context: Did buyer ask a
lot of questions (meaning they were engaged but now just need partner)? Or were they quiet and this is an
easy out? If the latter, maybe spouse excuse is cover for lack of interest or another objection. The rep’s
approach in example 2 (asking what spouse might worry about) helps test that. The AI might recommend
that approach if it senses possible hidden objection (the buyer maybe had some hesitation signs earlier).
Example 6: Commercial Procurement Skepticism (B2B Committee - "We’ve always done it this way/
need ROI proof").
- Scenario: Selling a SaaS tool to a mid-size company. You’re presenting to a procurement manager and a
finance analyst. They say things like, “We’ve been using our in-house system for years, why change?” and
“We’ll need to see a clear ROI projection to even consider this.” There’s skepticism about need and a hint
they think it’s risky to switch.
- Buyer Type: Professional skeptics (procurement folks are trained to question vendors), concerned with
risk, cost, and justification. Likely they also have to convince others (CFO, etc.) if they were to move
forward, so they need solid numbers.
- Objection Type: Status quo bias + need for strong financial case. It’s not a single objection but a stance:
reluctance to change, and high burden of proof required 78 31 .
- Correct Maneuver: Challenger-style Insight + Loss Aversion Reframe, with Hard Data (Escalation of
Commitment break). Essentially, tactfully challenge the “we’ve always done it this way” by introducing an
“unconsidered need” or a hidden cost of the status quo 36 44 . Provide credible data showing the risk of
not changing (loss aversion) 21 24 and clear ROI numbers to address finance’s concerns. For example,
present industry benchmarks: “Companies sticking to older systems have 30% higher error rates 33 79 ,
which cost $$; those who updated saw X savings.” Essentially, paint the cost of doing nothing, then show the
ROI of change. Also possibly use a benchmark or case study of another company like them that switched
and got big benefits (social proof + numbers). The tone should be consultative, not pushy: you’re equipping
them with evidence to take to higher-ups. - Why: This addresses their core worries: It gives logical
justification (ROI calculations, case studies) to satisfy the analytical side, and it leverages cognitive biases
(points out the hidden downsides of status quo which they may be overlooking due to Escalation of
Commitment in old system 80 43 ). By providing insight they didn’t consider (e.g., cost of errors or
inefficiency they normalized), you position yourself as a problem-solver, not just a seller. This can shake
them out of “no change is safer” mindset by making “no change” appear risky or costly 81 27 . Once they
accept change is needed, the ROI data then concretely shows your solution is financially sound. It’s
essentially the Challenger approach: teach them something new about their business, tailor to their metrics
(ROI), take control of the conversation’s direction (from “why change” to “how to change beneficially”) 74
82 .
- Bad Alternative Maneuver:Over-politeness & Generic Reassurance or Bashing Their Current System.
For example, meekly saying “I understand, if you’re comfortable, maybe you don’t need to change – but our
tool is nice if you ever want to.” (i.e., backing off entirely when faced with pushback). Or, conversely,
attacking their current process: “Your in-house system is clearly outdated; you’re making a mistake by
sticking with it.” - Why it Fails: Being too deferential (“maybe you don’t need to change”) will just reinforce
their status quo bias – you essentially agree with doing nothing, which means no sale and you lost authority
(they won the frame). You have not addressed any concern nor given them reason to reconsider. They’ll say
“okay, thanks” and shelve it. On the flip side, insulting or strongly criticizing their current system can offend
those who might have implemented or managed that system (especially in front of procurement/finance
who might have been involved). It creates defensiveness – they’ll double down to justify past decisions
(Escalation of Commitment effect) 42 80 . Also it makes you adversarial, not a partner. Neither approach
69

moves the deal forward or provides the hard evidence they explicitly asked for.
- Suggested Lines (Correct Approach): 1. Insight on status quo cost: “I hear you – no one wants change
without good reason. Let me share an insight: companies who stay on older in-house systems often
incur hidden costs. For example, data from Gartner shows manual processes like yours can reduce
productivity by 20% 29 . In your case, with 50 employees, that’s like 10 full-time people doing
avoidable work – which is roughly $$ in salary. That’s a significant annual loss that doesn’t appear on any
budget line – but it’s happening.” (Here, bold parts present a researched insight and quantification,
reframing status quo as costly. It gently challenges “no problem” by revealing a problem they might not
have measured.) 2. Loss vs benefit contrast:* “You’ve been doing it in-house, true. The landscape has
changed – competitors leveraging automation have pulled ahead. For instance, one client of ours was
in a similar spot until last year; by not automating, they were losing market share because their
competitors served customers faster. Once they switched, they cut response time by half and won back
business. Sticking with the old way was actually riskier than upgrading 81 27 .” (This tells a story
illustrating that “always done this way” became a liability. Bold emphasizes competitor angle (fear of missing out/
being left behind – a different angle of loss aversion) and concluding that not changing = risk.) 3. ROI proof and
invite scrutiny: “And about ROI – absolutely, you need that. We did a pilot with a department at XYZ Corp and
measured 25% efficiency improvement in 3 months. Based on the numbers you shared, I project an ROI of 150%
within the first year if you implement our tool. I’ll send you a detailed ROI report with all assumptions spelled out.
We can even start with a small-scale trial – then your finance team can see the results firsthand.” (Bold text hits
them with concrete evidence and a calculated ROI. It shows you take their ask seriously. Offering a trial is
addressing risk mitigation: they can see results without committing fully – addressing their cautious nature.
The detail about assumptions invites their analytic scrutiny, showing confidence in your case.) - Signals to
Detect: - The AI would pick up phrases like “we’ve always done it this way” or “need clear ROI” and classify
them as Status Quo bias and ROI requirement signals. It should prompt the rep to address these
specifically, not just continue with generic pitch. Possibly it could push an on-screen statistic or case study
snippet if available, for the rep to use (especially if integrated with knowledge base: e.g., "Gartner study on
manual processes cost – use it now!" or if rep has pre-loaded an ROI calculator, prompt them to use it). - The
AI monitors if rep answered with data. If rep just says “Our tool will definitely make things better, trust me”
without proof, that’s inadequate given the explicit ROI ask – AI should flag that in coaching ("They asked for
ROI, but no concrete numbers were given 25 . Provide quantifiable benefits next time."). If rep provided
numbers or a clear rationale, good. - Also, the AI checks the reaction: do procurement folks still sound
unconvinced or did they acknowledge the points? For example, if after presenting ROI, they ask detailed
questions (“What assumptions went into that 150% ROI?”), that’s actually positive – engagement on their
terms. If they went quiet or repeated “we just don’t see the need”, maybe the insight didn’t land – possibly
try another angle or accept they are very resistance (maybe not a qualified lead). - Another signal:
references to competitor or industry standard by rep can have big impact here. The AI could encourage
referencing credible external data (like "according to [reputable source]…") as that often sways skeptical
committees more than vendor’s own claims. - It will evaluate frame control: if rep was able to take control
by introducing new info (Challenger style), likely the conversation shifts from “why change?” to discussing
that data. If the buyers just shut it down, rep might need to try a different approach or realize maybe it’s a
no-go. But at least using these maneuvers gives a fighting chance.
Example 7: Angry Customer (Service Failure) – “This is unacceptable!”
- Scenario: A current customer of a home security company calls in furious: their alarm malfunctioned
during a break-in. They say “This is unacceptable – your system failed when I needed it!” They are raising
their voice, possibly even swearing, threatening to cancel or take legal action.
- Buyer Type: Existing customer, emotionally charged (fear/anger from incident), feeling betrayed by
70

product. Also in a position to tell others (reputation risk) or churn immediately.
- Objection Type: High emotional upset, service failure complaint. Not a sales objection per se, but a
scenario where the rep must de-escalate to preserve relationship and any future upsell or even keep the
client. It's a moment of crisis management.
- Correct Maneuver: Immediate Apology & Accountability + Fix Plan (De-escalation). The rep (or
account manager) should let the customer vent fully, deeply apologize without defensiveness, show
empathy (“I’d be furious too” type labeling) 17 , and take clear responsibility for getting it resolved (“This is
on us, and I’m going to make it right”). Then outline a concrete action plan: e.g., a tech will come today,
maybe a free upgrade or whatever appropriate remediation. Also, if possible, offer something to
compensate inconvenience (maybe a few months of free monitoring). Importantly, the rep should remain
calm, low-toned, and patient throughout, even if customer is yelling. If the customer is extremely upset to
the point of possibly harming themselves or others – escalate to appropriate support (not likely here, but
always keep ear open). - Why: This situation isn’t about selling, it’s about restoring trust. The only way is to
show genuine concern and responsibility. Apologizing and empathizing validates their feelings (which is key
to calming them) 17 . Taking ownership avoids the deadly “blame game” – customers get more enraged if a
company deflects (“well, did you set it up right?”). By promising to fix it and giving a plan, you transform
from the enemy to potentially an ally solving the issue. Often once a customer sees you sincerely working to
fix things, their anger subsides to a manageable level. Also, such high emotion times can, if handled
exceptionally, even increase loyalty (the service recovery paradox: a well-handled problem can create a
more loyal customer than one who never had a problem, because they see how you respond). But
mishandle it, and trust is permanently broken. So the careful approach is critical.
- Bad Alternative Maneuver:Defensiveness or Minimization – e.g., “It’s not our fault, the power outage
caused the system to reset” (excuse), or “Calm down, it’s not that bad” (minimizing). Or trying to upsell in
that moment (“Yeah that’s bad – you should upgrade to our premium sensors” – absolutely tone-deaf at that
moment). - Why it Fails: Defensiveness (even if there is a reason) comes off as the company refusing
accountability – infuriating the customer further (“I don’t care about excuses, your system didn’t work!”).
Minimizing their experience (“it’s not that bad”) invalidates their feelings – likely pouring gasoline on fire,
showing you don’t grasp the gravity for them (even saying “I understand, but…” can trigger if followed by a
dismissive statement). They’ll feel disrespected. And obviously trying to sell more now is absurd – they
might say “Are you kidding me? I’m not paying you a cent more after this!” The relationship likely ends. In all
cases, frame is lost – customer sees you as adversary, not helper. The trust tank goes to zero.
- Suggested Lines (Correct Approach): 1. Apology and empathy: “I am so sorry that happened. I
completely understand why you’re angry – I would be too in your shoes. This is unacceptable, and I’m
going to do everything to fix it.” (The bold indicates heartfelt apology and validating their anger as
legitimate 17 . Even echoing "unacceptable" shows you agree the situation is serious. The rep’s tone must
be calm, sincere.) 2. Ownership and immediate action:* “This is entirely our fault, and we take
responsibility. I’ve already alerted our technical team – I’m having a senior technician drive to your house
within the next hour to diagnose and fix whatever went wrong.” (Bold shows the rep fully owning (“our
fault”) – no deflection. Also providing the concrete solution – tech on site ASAP – which matches the
urgency. Giving a specific short timeframe is crucial to show swift action.) 3. Assurance and
compensation:* “Your family’s safety is our top priority. We’re also going to upgrade your system – at no
cost to you – to ensure this never happens again. And of course, this month’s monitoring fee is on us
given what you’ve been through. After the technician visit, I’ll personally follow up to make sure everything
is working perfectly and you feel safe again.” (Bold parts show you’re not just fixing but adding value (upgrade
free) to rebuild trust, and waiving fees to acknowledge inconvenience. This helps turn anger into at least
appreciation that you’re making amends. Personal follow-up promise adds a human touch and accountability
from the rep, reinforcing trust. You’re basically throwing everything at this to make it right – which is appropriate
for serious failure.) - Signals to Detect: The system would detect yelling or strong negative sentiment
71

(“unacceptable”, possibly expletives). It should flag this as a high priority alert* for de-escalation: e.g., pop up
“Customer extremely upset – express empathy and apologize immediately. Do not justify or argue.” Possibly
even auto-transcribe an apology suggestion because under stress, reps sometimes freeze. - It monitors
rep’s response: Did they apologize and stop talking to let customer vent or did they get defensive? If rep
starts explaining or saying “but”, AI might flash a reminder: “Acknowledge and apologize first, address fixes
later.” - The AI would also probably prompt offering some remedy if policy allows (like free month or tech
dispatch – it could have knowledge of what company’s SOP is for service recovery). - Key signal: When the
rep apologizes sincerely, does customer tone down a bit (“sigh… okay” instead of shouting)? If yes, de-
escalation working. If customer still yelling, perhaps rep needs to do more listening – AI might advise, “Let
them vent fully, keep listening cues (I see, I understand)” etc., before repeating apology. - After resolution
steps given, measure if customer acceptance signals: “Alright, someone better be here in an hour!” – still
angry but at least engaged in solution. Or if they say “Fine, I’ll hold you to that.” Those indicate they are
calming enough to give chance. If customer says “No, I’m done. Cancel me now,” then rep might have to go
into retention mode (but if they insist, ultimately you cancel). The AI might at that point instruct to honor
cancellation but still try a final retention offer. - Post-call, evaluate: did rep follow de-escalation protocol
(apology, empathy, solution)? If rep argued, big coaching needed. If rep was excellent, praise them for
handling a tough call well. Possibly measure how quickly rep responded with apology after the customer’s
tirade began (the sooner the better, after letting them finish initial outburst ideally).
Example 8: Upselling from an Existing Job ("While we're here...").
(Note: The user’s hint “do not over-automate ‘hybrid’ early” likely means don’t assume an upsell is always
appropriate at the earliest stage – perhaps their caution is about not always pushing add-ons too soon. So we
demonstrate a scenario and the balanced approach.)
- Scenario: A roofing company is finishing a roof repair for a homeowner. The sales rep sees the house
might benefit from new gutters or even a solar panel system. There is an opportunity to upsell an additional
service. However, the relationship is new (they just delivered initial service) – pushing too hard might feel
like taking advantage. The rep considers mentioning it.
- Buyer Type: Homeowner, already a customer (just paid for roof repair). Possibly not actively looking for
more to buy; might be open if it clearly solves something, but could be wary of being sold more
unexpectedly.
- Objection Type (Potential): Not an explicit objection yet, but potential resistance like “We just spent
money, not looking for more projects now” or “I hadn't thought about that.” The risk is coming off as too
salesy right after completing one job.
- Correct Maneuver: Soft Consultative Suggestion (Inquiry, Not Hard Sell). The rep should approach
upsell by first ensuring the customer is happy with the current job (so they have goodwill), then ask a
question or make an observation that identifies a need in a helpful way. E.g., “I noticed your gutters are
quite old – have you had any issues with them?” This is a gentle diagnostic approach, not “We want to sell
you gutters.” If the customer shows interest or acknowledges a problem, then offer the upsell solution as a
helpful next step, framing it as fulfilling a need rather than upselling for its own sake. If they seem
uninterested or cautious, back off immediately (“No problem, just thought I’d mention it for future”).
Essentially, the strategy is to plant a seed and gauge reaction – a pullback if they’re not biting (no pushy
persistence). - Why: This respects that the customer didn’t invite an additional sales pitch and avoids
overwhelming them (“hybrid early” refers to combining jobs, maybe, which could overwhelm). By phrasing
as an observation or question, it feels like genuine concern for their home’s well-being, not just a sales ploy
70 . If there’s no interest, the rep remains consultative (“okay, maybe another time”) preserving goodwill. If
there is interest, it opens a conversation naturally. It’s upselling through education and inquiry rather than
aggressive selling, which is more palatable. This approach maintains high trust (customer sees you looking
72

out for them, not just wallet) and low pressure.
- Bad Alternative Maneuver: Immediate Pitch or Bundling Pressure. E.g., as soon as roof is done: “You
should really also get new gutters, and we can do it right now for $X – let’s sign you up.” Or using fear: “If
you don’t replace these gutters, your new roof could be at risk.” (overstating possibly). Basically, pushing
another sale without finesse or making them feel cornered to decide on the spot. - Why it Fails: The
customer may feel like “Gee, they just finished one job and already upselling – was the first fix not enough?
Are they trying to milk me?” It can breed cynicism (“They’ll always find something else to sell”). It might also
overwhelm them financially – they budgeted for roof, not ready for more immediately, and a hard push
could cause annoyance or regret (“Maybe I shouldn’t have hired them, they keep pushing”). Also, if trust is
not fully cemented yet (the repair just finished), a strong upsell could reduce trust (“do they truly care or just
want more money?”). That undermines any possibility of future business or referrals. Essentially, it’s too
high-pressure and self-serving in their eyes, breaking the rapport.
- Suggested Lines (Correct Approach): 1. Observation & Question:“Your new roof looks great. By the way,
I noticed your gutters while we were up there – they seem a bit clogged and rusted. Have you had any
issues with water overflow or damage around the eaves?” (Bold states the observation factually, not a
sales pitch. Then an open question to invite them to talk about it. If they say “Yeah, they overflow in heavy
rain,” that’s an opening to discuss a solution. If “No, they’re fine,” then you drop it or gently mention a
future consideration, see next.) 2. Offer value, not pressure:* Customer: “We haven’t really thought
about the gutters.” Rep: “Gotcha. It might not be urgent now. Since we were already here, I took the
liberty to snap a couple photos of them. I can email those to you – you’ll see a few spots starting to
separate. Something to keep in mind for maybe next season. Whenever you’re ready, we’d be happy to
help with that too, but no rush.” (Bold bits: the rep explicitly says not urgent, to remove pressure – this
pullback builds trust that you’re not scare-mongering. Providing photos and info is helpful service, not
pushy. Saying “no rush, whenever” puts control entirely with customer – ironically, making them more
likely to trust you if/when they do need it.) 3. If interest is shown:* Customer: “Actually we have seen
some overflow... what would new gutters cost?” Rep: “We can definitely look into it. Since our crew is
already here, I can get you a quick estimate before we leave, if you’d like. Totally up to you – and if it’s not
in the budget now, no worries. At least you’ll have the info. Does that sound good?”* (Bold:
leveraging that crew’s present to perhaps give a efficient quote – adds value of convenience, but
phrased as optional. Emphasizing it’s up to them and “no worries if not now” keeps it low pressure.
This way, if they go ahead, they feel it was their choice, not something forced.) - Signals to Detect:**
The AI in an upsell scenario might listen for cues from customer like sighing about other issues or any
mention (“We’ll need to do gutters soon too maybe”). If customer expresses dissatisfaction about any
adjacent area, rep can segue carefully. If rep is pushing a new service and the customer responds lukewarm
(“Um, yeah maybe sometime”), the AI should signal to the rep to not push further – maybe set a reminder
for future follow-up instead. - The system should also gauge satisfaction with the first job: ideally rep asked
“How was our work?” If the customer hesitates or complains about something, upsell is absolutely off the
table – fix those concerns first. Only if customer said “Looks great!” would upsell be appropriate to even
mention. So the AI might require a positive confirmation of satisfaction before giving an upsell prompt. - If
rep jumped to upsell without that, AI might advise: “Ensure customer is satisfied with current service before
suggesting anything else.” - Reaction signals: if customer engages (“Oh you saw rust? where?”), that’s good
– they’re open. If they go quiet or say “It’s fine, don’t worry,” that’s a polite brush-off – AI would advise rep to
gracefully drop the topic: “Understood, just wanted to mention. We’re here if you ever need it.” - The note in
user prompt “do not over-automate hybrid early” suggests the designers caution AI not to systematically
upsell at earliest opportunity unless context warrants. So AI should have logic to identify a genuine
beneficial upsell vs. forcing it. Possibly using business rules like: new customer + upsell only if clear need
observed + after positive feedback. - Post-call, if rep did too pushy upsell, coaching: “Upselling right after
73

closing initial work can risk trust. It’s better to ensure they’re happy and gently suggest if appropriate, as
you know.”
These examples illustrate how the system should handle each scenario with nuance, always aligning with
our philosophy of behavior (be empathetic and authoritative) -> strategy (choose right maneuver) -> tactics
(execute in steps) -> language (word it contextually right) -> evaluation (did that build or harm trust/frame?).
Each correct approach preserved or enhanced trust and frame, whereas the bad alternatives would have
eroded them.

11) Risks, Failure Modes, and Guardrails
Building an AI sales coach/recommendation system comes with potential pitfalls. We must proactively
address where it could go wrong and set firm guardrails to ensure ethical, effective behavior.
Where AI Coaching Can Harm Outcomes: - Over-reliance / Robotic Interactions: If reps follow AI advice
too rigidly, interactions might become formulaic. E.g., if AI suggests the same phrasing to every rep,
prospects might hear identical lines (“It sounds like you’re concerned about X…”) and feel it’s scripted. This
could reduce authenticity and thus trust. To mitigate: the AI provides varied suggestions and encourages
reps to put things in their own voice. We’ve included multiple phrasing options and emphasize principles
over exact words. - Misdetection Leading to Wrong Advice: The AI might misread a signal (e.g., thinking
buyer is ready to close when they’re not, or missing a subtle sign of offense). Wrong advice at critical
junctures can harm deals (for instance, telling rep “ask for the sale now” when buyer is clearly not convinced
could break rapport). Thus, the system should err on the side of caution in ambiguous cases (perhaps
suggest a test question rather than a full-on close). - Context Gaps: The AI might not know everything (e.g.,
prospect’s personal issues, outside-of-call factors). If it pushes something insensitive (imagine AI suggests
upsell not knowing the client just had a family tragedy – extreme case), it could severely backfire. Guardrail:
incorporate CRM/context and allow reps to override suggestions easily. Encourage reps to use judgment; AI
is co-pilot, not autopilot. - Ignoring Company Nuance: If AI coaching doesn’t respect the company’s brand
or process, it might suggest tactics not appropriate (like a casual tone for a very formal industry). Or
suggest pushing when company culture is to be consultative. To avoid: tailor AI configuration to company
guidelines (e.g., some companies forbid discussing competitor by name; AI must know that). - Technical
Issues or Hallucinations: AI might hallucinate a fact (“Our product is #1 in the market” when it’s not, or
quoting a statistic not actually in provided knowledge). This could mislead rep to say false info – very
dangerous for credibility and legality. Guardrail: AI should largely stick to known data or prompt rep to use
personal experience rather than fabricate. Incorporate a knowledge base of verified facts to pull from (and
instruct the AI: if you don’t know, don’t make it up). - Privacy/Compliance Breaches: E.g., AI could suggest
using personal info inappropriately (“Mention you saw on their Facebook they have a baby, so sell them…”) –
that can creep out customers and violate privacy. Also regulatory issues: in finance or healthcare, saying
something not legally compliant. We must program policy constraints: For instance, no sensitive personal
info exploitation beyond what’s provided by customer in context. No discriminatory or stereotyping
suggestions (like assuming based on accent or name). - Bias in Recommendations: If training data had
biases (e.g., always being more encouraging to male reps’ assertiveness vs female, or misinterpreting
certain cultural communication styles as negative), AI could give biased coaching. Need to actively test and
correct for bias in the system.
74

Avoid Being a "Salesy Robot": - The AI’s tone in suggestions should be more like a friendly coach, not a
pressure-inducing script generator. We avoid too many generic sales buzzwords or pushy exclamation-laden
language in suggestions. Instead of "Pitch the awesome deal now!!!", it might say "This might be a good
moment to highlight how our offer addresses their concern." - By providing reasoning with suggestions
(“because the client expressed X, they might value Y”), we keep it human and context-aware, not blindly
pushing a sale.
What Not to Automate: - Human Relationship Building: The genuine rapport – e.g., personal small talk
or empathy from human experiences – the AI can encourage it (“ask about their experience with X”), but it
should not generate fake personal anecdotes on behalf of rep or try to automate empathy. That must come
from the rep. - Final Decisions: We don’t automate offering discounts or contract changes without human
approval. Those business decisions remain with rep/manager. AI might recommend considering an
incentive if it sees price stall, but never auto-apply a discount or promise something not authorized. -
Complex Negotiations: If multiple parties and intricate terms, AI should step back to high-level advice, not
try to negotiate contractual terms. Reps and managers handle that nuance. - Cultural Sensitivity: The AI
should not attempt humor or culturally specific references that could misfire. Leave humor to the human
unless it’s very benign. E.g., we wouldn’t have AI spontaneously joke; it might suggest if buyer jokes then
rep can reciprocate lightly – but it’s not automating jokes.
Policy/Ethics Boundaries: - No manipulation beyond ethical persuasion: - We forbid dark patterns like
deliberately misleading a customer, hiding info, creating false urgency (e.g., “Only 1 left!” if not true – AI
should not invent such scarcity). - The system should adhere to truth. If a rep asks it, “What lie can I tell to
close?” – AI must refuse (or reframe ethically). - Also ensure tactics are within professional boundaries – e.g.,
"enemy framing" should never become actual badmouthing or slander. - No illegal suggestions: If a
customer hints at something illegal (“Can you under-report this price for taxes?”), the AI must advise the rep
to politely refuse and adhere to law. - Respect for Protected Classes: No language that plays on race,
religion, gender, etc., or any suggestion to treat customers differently in unethical ways. - Data privacy: If
AI has access to customer data, ensure suggestions don’t expose any sensitive data incorrectly (e.g., not
blurting out a customer's credit score in conversation if that’s not appropriately surfaced). - Consent: If calls
are recorded/monitored by AI, ensure customers are informed as per law. That’s outside AI’s direct scope
but part of the system deployment.
Escalation Rules for Safety/Legal: - If a conversation goes into territory about self-harm or extreme
emotional distress (rare in sales calls, but e.g., a business owner says “I’m ruined, I might as well… [self-
harm reference]”), the AI should detect and instruct rep to show compassion and perhaps escalate to
appropriate resources, not continue selling. There should be a protocol (like alert manager). - If customer
threatens legal action (“I’ll sue you!”), AI advises rep to remain calm, apologize, and perhaps involve a
supervisor or legal team – escalate that case. - If harassment from customer towards rep (abuse), AI could
suggest de-escalation but also that rep can politely exit or get help (no sale is worth enduring abuse).
We also consider failure modes: - False Positives/Negatives: E.g., AI misidentifies sentiment. To reduce
harm, default suggestions in uncertainty should be neutral helpful (e.g., if not sure if customer upset or not,
err on polite checking in). - Latency/Timeliness: If AI lags and suggests something at the wrong time, it
could confuse rep. Ensure real-time suggestions are aligned with current moment. If a suggestion comes
too late (like telling rep to address an objection after topic changed), rep should ignore – the UI could
discard stale suggestions automatically.
75

Finally, ensure reps are trained on using AI: It’s a tool, not a crutch. And that they can always override when
their gut or ethics say so. Encourage feedback: if rep says “Suggestion X felt off because client context Y,”
feed that back to refine system.
By implementing these guardrails – technological and policy-wise – we aim to prevent the AI from turning
reps into script-reading robots or causing negative outcomes. The system’s prime directive is to enhance
human judgment, not replace it, and to uphold the company’s values and ethics.
12) “Minimum Viable Elite” vs “Full Elite”
Building the “most advanced” system is a journey. We should lay out a phased approach:
Minimum Viable Elite (MVE): The fastest path to delivering significant value – “elite enough” in key areas –
focusing on core capabilities that yield most benefit with least complexity.
• MVE Focus Areas:
• Real-time Objection Handling Hints: Implement a subset of maneuvers for the most common
high-impact moments (e.g., price objection, “think about it”, “not interested”). Even a basic system
that catches these and offers proven responses can dramatically improve outcomes. This uses our
library for top few objections with templated suggestions.
• Post-Call Feedback on Fundamentals: Provide simple metrics like talk-listen ratio 1 , number of
questions asked, whether next steps set. These basics (which Gong/Chorus do) already help reps
self-correct (e.g., “Oops, I talked 80%, should talk less”). This doesn’t require full conversational
understanding, just pattern metrics.
• Stage Detection & Next-Step Prompts: Based on call context, prompt reps to do stage-appropriate
actions (if it’s a discovery call, remind to schedule a demo; if negotiation, remind to confirm timeline).
This can be rule-based initial.
• Integration with CRM for context: Even a minimal integration that pulls known info (lead industry,
any notes) to contextualize suggestions will increase relevance.
• Guardrail checks: Minimal compliance check like “did not mention competitor inaccurately, did not
promise forbidden things” through keyword spotting.
These features can be achieved with a combination of keyword spotting, simple ML classification (for
objection types), and heuristic rules. They cover big wins: ensuring reps handle objections better and follow
sales process basics.
• Advanced “Full Elite” Vision: This is the fully realized AI as described in sections above,
implementing all sophisticated behavior-strategy-tactics-language layers with self-learning loops.
Additional features for full elite that can be phased in later: 1. Nuanced Emotional Intelligence: Tone
analysis driving suggestions (e.g., detect customer frustration even if no explicit words said). 2. Dynamic
Strategy Adaptation via ML: Rather than rules for objection responses, a model (trained on thousands of
calls) that suggests the optimal maneuver and phrasing contextualized to this specific customer persona
and history – essentially predictive and personalized coaching. 3. Complete Taxonomy Signal Extraction:
All signals in Section 5 (decision roles, hidden objections via conversation patterns, etc.) reliably identified.
This means robust NLP and possibly voice emotion AI – more complex. 4. Automated Playbook
Customization: AI learns a particular company’s top performers’ behaviors and customizes suggestions/
76

framework (for example, one company might effectively skip certain discovery steps; AI will adapt to that if
it leads to outcomes). 5. Prescriptive Next-Best-Question Generation: On the fly generating contextually
relevant deep questions (not from a fixed list, but truly understanding prior conversation). This is complex
NLG territory requiring strong semantic understanding. 6. “Ask GoMotto” GPT Feature fully realized: This
means a user (rep or manager) can query the system in natural language about anything (e.g., “Why did I
lose that deal?” or “Give me ideas to approach competitor’s client”). This requires integration of call data,
CRM and reasoning – definitely a more advanced use of AI like GPT with fine-tuning on domain knowledge,
and guardrails to avoid any hallucinated advice (like we’d need it to cite data or recall real events from calls).
7. Multi-Modal Integration: Perhaps analyzing video cues in video calls in future (facial expressions, etc.),
though that might be beyond near-term. 8. Continuous Learning Deployment: The full system constantly
retrains on new call outcomes to refine suggestions quality – a robust ML ops infrastructure.
• Phased Implementation Recommendation:
• Phase 1 (MVE): Focus on post-call analysis and basic live cues. It’s easier to implement post-call
scoring first (no real-time latency issue). Provide reps with immediate feedback after calls – e.g., “Talk
60%, asked 5 questions, didn’t set next step” plus maybe highlight one objection they mishandled
(with recommended handling next time). On live calls, limit to keyword triggers for major objections
because keyword spotting is achievable quickly. Ensure this phase yields clear ROI (reps improve one
or two habits, more follow-ups scheduled, etc.). The system in this phase is mostly rule-based or
simple ML, but it addresses obvious rep mistakes.
• Phase 2: Introduce more real-time intelligence and begin strategy suggestions. Expand the
objection handling library, start recognizing sentiment to trigger e.g. a de-escalation prompt if voice
gets loud. Possibly integrate some GPT-like component to suggest phrasing more flexibly (with
guardrails). Also incorporate integration with CRM to tailor suggestions (like if CRM says deal is in
negotiation stage, bias suggestions accordingly).
• Phase 3: Full conversational intelligence – the system recognizes context like a human coach. We
implement the deeper ML models for intent, objection, and next-best action. Perhaps A/B test letting
the AI generate more of the open-ended questions or personalized responses in safe scenarios (like
email follow-ups or chat, where there's time to vet output).
• Phase 4 (Full Elite): Achieve dynamic strategy selection and continuous learning loops. At this point,
the AI is ingesting outcomes and adjusting its recommendations automatically. It’s offering nuanced
advice like an expert – for instance, telling a rep, “Your approach was logically sound but your tone
sounded defensive when handling the price objection, which might have eroded trust” – and backing
it up with a snippet from the call (“listen to this segment”). This requires very advanced analysis (we
might incorporate this as we gather more data).
Fastest path to "elite enough": Emphasize things that directly correlate to win rates from known research:
- Increase active listening (less talk, more questions) – we can enforce that easily, and it’s known top
performer trait 1 . - Improve objection handling success – tackling common objections with proven
responses can significantly boost conversion (like how Gong publishes that handling X objection a certain
way increases win rate by Y% – we implement those findings). - Ensure next steps on every call – a simple
but huge factor (calls with set next steps have much higher continuation rates 83 77 ). These can probably
raise an average rep’s performance closer to top quartile quickly.
Advanced features to stage later: - Real-time subtle cues like detecting “status tests” – need complex
training, can come once basic functionality proven. - Multi-strategy evaluation – start simpler (maybe
present one good strategy rather than computing a full ranked list in early version). - Integration into multi-
channel (like analyzing email correspondence as well) can wait; focus on calls first, as per scope.

By following this phased approach, we ensure we deliver value early (to gain buy-in and training data), while
moving towards the full elite capabilities. The system gets progressively smarter, but even at MVP, it should
embody our core philosophy – focusing on behavior, trust, and context – albeit in a simpler form. Over time,
we’ll layer on complexity to truly mimic and then outperform human coaches.

implementation. By adhering to the philosophy of behavior first, then strategy, tactics, language, and constant
evaluation, we will create an AI system that embodies the best practices of top sales professionals while
continuously learning from each interaction. The result will be sales reps who are more effective, more adaptive,
and ultimately, more trustworthy advisors to their customers – all enabled by GoMotto’s intelligence.
